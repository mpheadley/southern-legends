Drive into Oneonta on a slow Saturday and the first thing you notice is that the old brick downtown is still standing, and still busy. The historic commercial strip along 1st Avenue runs about two and a half blocks, most of it early-1900s storefronts that never got torn down for a strip mall. In a lot of small Alabama towns, that stretch would be half-empty. Here it is the front porch of the county seat, and folks still gather on it.

Oneonta has carried the "Alabama Community of Excellence" label for a while, and the town wears it like a work shirt, not a trophy. This is Blount County, the covered-bridge country in Murphree's Valley, about thirty-five miles up the road from Birmingham. Close enough to feel the city's pull. Far enough to keep its own name.

The bigger story right now is a mile from the square. The old Blount County–Oneonta Agri-Business Center, a building that served the community for forty years and then aged out of usefulness, is being rebuilt into something the county has never had. HomeTown Sports is a 75,000-square-foot regional complex on nine acres, and the plans are not small. Four basketball courts or six volleyball courts or sixteen pickleball courts on one convertible floor. A turf field. Team rooms, a food-truck courtyard, RV parking, and seating for two to three thousand people. The project's own site points to a Fall or Winter 2026 opening.

The part worth watching is buried in the amenities list: meeting spaces and conference rooms, built right into the complex. That is the piece Blount County has been missing. When you can seat a couple thousand people under one roof and still have breakout rooms down the hall, you stop being a town that sends its events to Birmingham and start being a town that hosts them.

Which is where a place like Oneonta could surprise people. The wedding trade is already here. Blount County draws couples to barns and lakeside pavilions that pull families in from three counties over. A region that already sells weddings has florists, bakers, photographers, and caterers scattered across it, and those vendors have nowhere central to show their work. A complex with real event space and easy parking is the kind of room a regional bridal show could actually fill.

None of this is a boom, and Oneonta would probably be the first to tell you so. It is something steadier. A town keeping its old storefronts, fixing up the building it outgrew, and leaving the lights on for whatever comes next.
