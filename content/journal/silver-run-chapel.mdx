There's a bell in Munford that still works. It hung over a one-room schoolhouse that served Talladega County starting in 1850, and when that building burned in 1907, the community rebuilt it the same year — same bell tower, same footprint, same ground. Stand on Bell Spring Road today and you can ring a bell that schoolchildren heard more than a century ago.

That kind of continuity is rare in this part of Alabama. We are good at losing buildings. A store closes, a church empties, a school consolidates, and the structure goes quiet, then soft, then gone. Silver Run Chapel is one of the ones that didn't go — and the reason it didn't is a person, not an accident.

## Renee's Twenty Years

Renee Rice bought the chapel in her early 40s, then had a baby, and still fully renovated the whole building. For about twenty years after that, she and Robbie kept it the way you'd keep a family table — friends married there, family gathered there, the occasional client booked it. [EDIT: confirm Robbie's relationship to Renee (spouse vs. sibling) and last name before publish.] It was never a full-time business. At one point they hired a team to run it as a venue, and that didn't work out. The chapel went quiet, but it stayed cared for, and the property at 62 Bell Spring Road is owned free and clear.

Renee's day job says a lot about how a person ends up carrying an 1850 bell for twenty years. She runs R&R Travel Service in Oxford, a Sandals-affiliate agency where she's spent years booking couples' honeymoons. A honeymoon booker who owns a wedding chapel. Some things line up like that. She also has an eye for places worth saving — she once sent me photos of the old CCC-built general store at Cheaha State Park because she wanted help imagining it restored. Nobody asked her to care about that. She just does.

## The Hour That Kept It

Here is where I come into it, and I'll be plain that I do: I run a bridal show, and I walked into Renee's travel office in Oxford one day to ask if she'd take a booth. Brian Howell had told me I should meet her. When I got there, Brian and his wife Rebecca were sitting right in front of her desk, booking a Sandals trip. A travel office behind a Wells Fargo, friends booking a vacation, and me with one small favor to ask.

So I asked it. Renee said yes to the booth. Then she said something I wasn't expecting: "If you can come back in an hour, I have an opportunity for you."

I came back in an hour. The opportunity was the chapel. She and Robbie had just listed it for sale — twenty years is a long time to carry a place, and they were ready to let it go. She was about to hand an 1850 bell and all that history to whoever showed up with a check, unless somebody who cared about weddings wanted to step in first.

I walked in asking for a booth. I walked out talking about a chapel.

## Where It Stands Now

The chapel didn't sell to a stranger. Renee still owns the property, but my wife Heather now owns and runs the Silver Run venue business, and I officiate there. Two families, one chapel that almost left the family — carried forward for couples instead of going quiet under a For Sale sign.

I've been writing ceremonies in Eastern Alabama for over a decade, and this room is honest in a way most venues aren't: a hundred chairs, hardwood floors, white walls, and light through tall windows the way it's come in since 1907. Nothing to hide behind. Couples walk in for a tour and get quiet — not intimidated quiet, settled quiet. This building has been a gathering place since before the Civil War, and that history takes the pressure off. You don't have to make the room feel meaningful. It got there first.

One acre. Nothing added that doesn't belong. A place where something true happened for a long time, and where couples now come to mark the truest day of theirs.
