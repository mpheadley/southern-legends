McClellan brought my dad to Oxford.

He was a Marine recruiter. The base needed them, the town needed the Marines, and so my family landed in Calhoun County the way a lot of families did — following an assignment, putting down roots in the kind of place that asks nothing of you except that you show up. He did. We stayed.

So I grew up knowing McClellan the way kids know a place that belongs to other people. I had friends whose families lived on base. I'd ride over and we'd hang out the way preteens do — nowhere specific, killing time, being twelve. In 1999, the fort was closing, and I remember walking through the PX with my friend while the shelves were half-empty and the whole place had that particular feeling of something that has already decided to be gone. We bought things I can't remember now. It felt like the last day of something.

Around that same time, my parents got a kit in the mail. An emergency kit. Instructions for what to do if something went wrong at the chemical weapons disposal facility. Because Anniston had been storing a significant piece of this country's chemical weapons stockpile for decades, and they were starting to incinerate them, and nobody was entirely sure what that meant for the people who lived nearby. My parents talked seriously about moving. We didn't. Most people didn't. You stay in a place until the place tells you to go, and even then, sometimes you stay anyway.

---

I keep coming back to the fact that this forest is alive because men were trying to blow it up.

Northwest of Anniston, on the old ranges of Fort McClellan, there is a stand of mountain longleaf pine, one of the last of its kind. Longleaf used to be most of Alabama. My part of the state was a sea of it. Then the timber crews came through and it went the way most good things go when there is money in cutting them down. Nearly all of it, gone in a generation.

Except here. And here it survived by accident, because the Army used this ground for artillery and the shells kept catching the woods on fire. Longleaf needs fire the way a marriage needs hard conversations. Burn off the clutter or the thing chokes. So while we were losing longleaf everywhere a person was being careful with it, we were keeping it in the one place nobody was careful at all.

I think about that a lot. How the thing that looks like destruction can be the thing that saves you. How a whole forest can hang on in the middle of a bombing range because the fire it feared was the fire it needed.

---

There is more up there than trees. German prisoners of war spent the last years of the Second World War on this ground and were treated with such decency that people called the camp an oasis. Some of them are buried here still. There is an amphitheater in the woods that once held a Joe Louis fight, a president, and one of the first integrated battalions this country ever fielded, in Alabama, before Alabama was ready for it. The plaque telling that story only came back in 2024. The story had been out there the whole time, growing quiet under the pines.

I did not grow up being told any of this. That is the thing about a place. It keeps its best stories low, down in the roots, and waits to see if you will kneel down and look.

The chemical weapons are gone now. The fort has been gone for twenty-five years. The longleaf is still there.

Some things survive by accident. Some survive because the fire found them before the quiet could.
