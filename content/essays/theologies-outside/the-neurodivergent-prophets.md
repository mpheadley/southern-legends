# The Neurodivergent Prophets

*Synthesized by Fable — 2026-08-21*

Everything feels amazing. So why is everybody telling me to slow down?

I posted a version of that question to Facebook on an August morning, along with a duck emoji, because I felt like a duck that day — paddling hard, gliding smooth, sun on the water. The comments came in gentle and worried. My wife had already been gentle and worried for a week. My psychiatrist would be gentle and worried within days. And I sat there with my phone in my hand, genuinely confused, because from inside my own head the evidence was overwhelming: I was fine. Better than fine. I was writing thousands of words a day. I was seeing connections between things nobody else could see. I felt close to God in a way I hadn't felt since seminary.

That's the thing nobody tells you about mania. It doesn't feel like sickness. It feels like revelation.

And here is where I have to be careful, because I'm about to say something that could be misheard in two opposite directions. So let me say both wrong versions first, so you can watch me walk between them.

Wrong version one: mania is just a disease, the feelings it produces are counterfeit, and any sense of divine closeness that arrives during an episode should be discarded like a bad check. This is the clinical reduction, and plenty of good doctors hold it, and I understand why. It keeps people safe. It also tells a whole population of us that the most spiritually vivid hours of our lives were chemical noise, and that God only speaks to the medicated and the calm.

Wrong version two: mania is a gift, a direct line, a prophetic anointing that the church and the pharmaceutical industry conspire to suppress. This one is seductive and it kills people. I have stood at the edge of it. The elevated state whispers that you don't need the pills, don't need the wife's worried face, don't need the doctor — that all those people are the crowd telling blind Bartimaeus to hush. Believing that whisper is not liberation. It's the disease writing its own theology.

The truth I've had to live my way into is stranger than either. The duck doesn't know he's manic. The duck feels amazing. And the duck is also, actually, a good duck. All three things are true at once, and any theology that can't hold all three has no room for people like me.

Now read your Bible with that in mind and see what happens.

Ezekiel lies on his side for three hundred and ninety days. Jeremiah says the word of God is a fire shut up in his bones, that he's tired of holding it in, that he cannot. John the Baptist lives in the desert eating insects and shouting at religious professionals. Hosea's whole marriage is a compulsive symbolic act. The prophets weep in public, strip in public, name their children as walking accusations. If any of these men walked into a church I served, someone would pull me aside after the service and ask, kindly, whether we should call somebody.

I am not diagnosing the prophets. I wasn't there, and retroactive diagnosis is a party trick, not scholarship. What I'm saying is smaller and harder: the people God used to say the most necessary things were, almost uniformly, people the sane and settled community found unbearable. The fire in the bones is a real experience. I've had it. Whatever you call its source, the prophetic tradition is a tradition of people whose inner weather was too big for the room, and the community's task was never to cure them into silence. It was to listen for God in them without pretending the cost wasn't real.

The church I served didn't know how to do that. To be fair, neither did I. We had two boxes: healthy and struggling. If you were healthy, you could lead. If you were struggling, we'd pray for you, which mostly meant we'd wait for you to become healthy so you could lead again. There was no box for a person whose struggle and whose gift came out of the same ground. There was no liturgy for the man who writes his best sermon at four in the morning because his brain will not stop singing, and who will pay for that sermon with a November he can't get out of bed.

So people like me learned to perform wellness. I preached about grace while hiding the thing in me that most needed it. And when the hiding finally failed — when the diagnosis came, when the denial cracked, when my wife stopped being gentle and started being scared — the institution had nothing for me but the second box. Struggling. Pray for him. Wait.

Here's what I believe now, from outside the gate. The denial was the disorder. Not the mania, not the depression — the pretending. The insistence that I was just productive, just passionate, just on fire for the Lord. Acceptance didn't erase the illness; it converted it. The same brain that once wrote grandiose plans at midnight now writes books. The same depths that once swallowed whole winters now let me sit with dying people and not flinch. I take my medication. I trust the people who watch my face. And I no longer believe God was absent from the elevated hours or present only in the level ones. God was in the whole weather system, the way God was in Jeremiah's fire and Elijah's collapse under the broom tree — because Elijah is in this story too, the prophet who ran a marathon on adrenaline and then begged to die, and God's response was not a rebuke. It was food, and sleep, and a quiet voice.

Food, sleep, a quiet voice, and then: go back, there's work.

That's the community I keep looking for. Not one that worships the fire, and not one that only extinguishes it. One that can say to its strange ones: we see the storm in you, and we see God in you, and we will not make you choose which one to admit to. Still a good duck. Hopeful duck, bipolar duck. Still a good duck.

The prophets were never the whole story, though. For every Jeremiah burning in public there was a Baruch — the quiet scribe who wrote it all down, who carried the scroll, who stayed. The gate has people on both sides of it, and the ones who wait there with us, holding the thread while we thrash, have a theology of their own. My wife could write it. In the next chapter, I'll try.
