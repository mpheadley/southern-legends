# Perichoresis

*Synthesized by Fable — 2026-08-21*

Everything felt amazing. That was the problem, or at least everybody kept telling me it was. Last August I posted a duck to Facebook — a hopeful duck, wings out, mid-waddle — with a caption that said, more or less, why is everyone telling me to slow down when I feel this good? Eleven words and an emoji, and I meant every one of them. I was manic. I didn't know it yet, or I knew it the way you know a storm is coming when the air goes green: a knowing you can talk yourself out of. And here is the part I need you to hold onto, because it's the part the church and the clinic both tend to drop: the duck was also a good duck. The mania was real and the goodness was real. Both things, at once, in the same bird.

I want to tell you about a word I learned in seminary that I've never once heard survive the trip into a pew. Perichoresis. It's an old Greek word the early church used to describe what goes on inside God — Father, Son, Spirit, not stacked in a hierarchy, not sitting in three separate chairs, but moving through one another. Indwelling each other. Some teachers have described it as a dance, and I'll take that, but the older sense is stranger and better: mutual containment without loss. Each person of the Trinity fully in the others, and nobody erased. Nobody absorbed. The love doesn't blur the persons into a smoothie. The distinctness is the point. The closeness is the point. Both, at once.

I'm not asking you to sign off on Trinitarian doctrine. If the word "Trinity" makes your shoulders climb toward your ears because of how it was wielded at you, set the doctrine down. Keep the picture. Because the picture is describing something you already know in your body, or something you've been starving for: what it would mean to be fully known by someone and not consumed. To be inside another person's life all the way, and still be yourself. Most of us have only ever gotten one or the other. Distance that preserved us, or intimacy that swallowed us. Perichoresis says the deepest reality in the universe is a love that does neither.

Here's where the duck comes back.

The night my wife finally said the word — manic — I heard it as an eviction. I heard: you are not who you think you are, the amazing feeling is a symptom, the ideas are a symptom, maybe you are a symptom. And I fought her, because if the mania was fake then it felt like I was fake, and I'd rather blow up the kitchen than be fake. That's the trap. When your sense of self and your illness are dancing that close, any hand reaching for the illness looks like a hand reaching for your throat.

What saved us — what is still saving us, present tense, this is not a testimony with a bow on it — was learning that she could be inside my episode without becoming it, and I could let her in without disappearing. She could say "this is mania" and it did not have to mean "you are nothing but mania." I could say "I hear you" without it meaning "I no longer exist." Two people, fully in each other's reality, neither one erased. It took a counselor, and medication, and a fight I'm not proud of, and a couple of weeks at my parents' house. It took acceptance, which I used to think meant surrender and now think means something closer to hospitality: letting the truth all the way into the house.

The old theologians said this mutual indwelling is what God is. Not what God does on a good day. What God is. Which means the ground floor of everything — under the atoms, under the sermons, under whatever went wrong for you at whatever church put you outside the gate — is a relationship where full presence and full personhood coexist. Nobody dominating. Nobody dissolving. If that's true, then every love you've experienced that respected your edges while refusing to abandon you was a small window into the engine room of reality. And every "love" that demanded you disappear — into a role, a doctrine, a marriage, an institution's idea of a good Christian — was a counterfeit, no matter how much God-language it wore.

I spent years as a pastor accidentally teaching the counterfeit. Die to self, we said, and we mostly meant: shrink. Be less. Sand yourself down until you fit. But God, if this old word is right, has never once asked anyone inside the divine life to be less of themselves. The Son doesn't shrink to make room for the Spirit. The making-room is the life. Which means the self you were told to kill — the loud one, the intense one, the one who feels everything at eleven — might not be the enemy of your spiritual life. It might be the guest of honor, needing not execution but a table with other people at it, people close enough to say hard true things and secure enough not to leave.

I am bipolar. That's the distinctness; I don't get to opt out of it. I am also held — by a wife who names the storm without renaming me, by a doctor, by kids who tell me the truth with the brutal mercy of children, by a God I still talk to even though I can't sit in most sanctuaries without my chest going tight. That's the indwelling. The diagnosis didn't erase the goodness and the goodness doesn't erase the diagnosis. Hopeful duck, bipolar duck. Still a good duck. I used to think that caption was a joke I made. Now I think it was the closest I've ever come to preaching perichoresis: everything true at once, nothing thrown away, the whole odd bird held.

But being held raises a harder question, one the duck can't answer. If God's own life is this dance of persons who never dominate each other — then what do we do with all the power the church actually used on us? That's where we're headed next: through the gate the powerful built, to the God who keeps walking out of it.
