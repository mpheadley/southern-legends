# The Limping Guide

*Synthesized by Fable — 2026-08-21*

Two days ago I posted a picture of a duck to Facebook. Under it I wrote: "Everything feels amazing. So, why everybody telling duck slow down?" I thought it was funny. It was funny. It was also my whole diagnosis in eleven words, and I half knew that when I typed it, which is the strangest part. The duck feels amazing. The duck is being told to slow down by everyone who loves him. The duck cannot figure out why, because from inside the duck, nothing is wrong. Everything is finally, gloriously right.

I have bipolar disorder. When the mania comes, it does not arrive looking like sickness. It arrives looking like clarity. Like calling. Like the fog of ordinary life burning off and the whole landscape of what God wants from me standing there in full sun. I have started ventures in that sunlight. I have written tens of thousands of words in it. I have also frightened my wife in it, and spent money we didn't have in it, and stood in my kitchen absolutely certain I was fine while the people who know me best told me, gently and then less gently, that I was not.

Here is what I want you to know before we go any further in this book: I am not writing to you from the far side of that. I am writing to you from inside it. Some of these chapters were drafted in elevated states and edited in low ones. I limp. That's not a confession I'm making so you'll trust me more, though maybe you will. It's the actual shape of the guide you've picked up.

The church I came out of had a different model of the guide. The guide stood at the front, whole and finished, and told you the way. He had been to the mountain, or at least he preached like he had. His marriage was strong, his doubts were resolved, his story arced cleanly from lost to found and stayed found. When he said "I struggled with this once," the operative word was once. The struggle was in the past tense, safely converted into a sermon illustration.

I tried to be that man. I stood in pulpits and did my best impression of someone who had arrived. And the whole time there was a current running under me that I did not have a name for yet, lifting me into seasons where I could preach like fire and work like three men, then dropping me into weeks where I could barely answer email. I called the high seasons anointing. I called the low seasons spiritual attack, or laziness, or failure. I did not call any of it what it was, because guides don't limp. Guides have arrived. If I admitted I hadn't, I thought, what exactly was I offering anyone?

It took losing the pulpit, and nearly losing more than that, to find the older answer sitting right there in the text I'd preached from for years. Jacob wrestles all night with someone he can't name, and he doesn't win, exactly. He holds on. He comes away with a blessing and a dislocated hip, and the story makes a point of telling us he limped for the rest of his life. The limp is not the price of the blessing. The limp is the proof of it. It's how you can tell he actually met something real in the dark, instead of just telling stories about it in the morning.

The guides I trust now all walk that way. Not one of them stands at the front of the room whole. They walk beside you, uneven, and when the path gets bad they don't say "I know the way" — they say "I've fallen here. Watch that spot." That's a different kind of authority. It's lower to the ground. It can't be faked from a stage, because it isn't a performance of having arrived. It's the visible record of having been somewhere.

I understand why the church prefers the other model. The finished guide is safer to follow and easier to fund. Nobody has to worry about him. But I think the finished guide is also why so many of us are outside the gate now. We sat in those rooms carrying our own unnamed currents — the depression we prayed about but never mentioned, the marriage held together with tape, the doubt that came home from the hospital with us — and we looked at the whole man at the front and drew the obvious conclusion. This is a place for finished people. We are not finished. Therefore this is not a place for us. Nobody said it out loud. Nobody had to.

So when I say I'm a limping guide, I'm not being modest. I'm telling you the terms of this book. I will not pretend to see farther than I see. When my own diagnosis is doing the talking, I will try to say so, and I have people around me — my wife, my doctor, my family — whose job is to catch it when I can't. That's part of the limp too: knowing the leg gives out, and building your life so that someone's there when it does. The duck who insists he doesn't need anyone telling him to slow down isn't free. He's just alone at high speed.

A few days after the first duck post, I wrote a second one. "Hopeful duck, bipolar duck. Still a good duck." I meant it as a joke again, and again it turned out to be theology. Because that's the whole wager of this book, right there. The diagnosis is real. The limp is permanent. And the goodness is not canceled by either one. Not goodness I earned by managing my condition well, or performed by hiding it — goodness the way Genesis means it, spoken over the creature before the creature has done anything at all. I spent years believing I had to choose: either I was well, or I was disqualified. Both of those were wrong, and the wrongness of them is most of why I'm outside the gate, and most of why I'm still talking to you from here.

Still a good duck. Hold onto that, because the next thing we have to talk about is the gate itself — who built it, what it was for, and why the people it keeps out so often look exactly like the people the whole story was written for.
