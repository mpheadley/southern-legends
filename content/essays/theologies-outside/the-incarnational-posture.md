# The Incarnational Posture

*Synthesized by Fable — 2026-08-21*

I stood in a hospital hallway once, wearing a stole I wasn't sure I still believed in, waiting to be let into a room where a man was dying. His daughter had called the church office because she didn't know who else to call. She hadn't been to a service in twenty years. She apologized for that, twice, before I even got my coat on. I want you to sit with that for a second — a woman watching her father die, and part of her energy was going toward apologizing to an institution for her attendance record.

I didn't fix that in the hallway. I couldn't. What I could do was go in, sit down, and stay. I held the man's hand. I didn't explain anything. I was just there, a body in a chair, breathing the same recycled hospital air, and when his daughter cried I didn't hand her a doctrine. I handed her the box of tissues from the windowsill, because that's what was true in the room.

That's the whole posture I want to talk about, and I almost hesitate to give it a theological name, because the name is exactly the kind of insider word I promised not to use. So let me build it from the ground instead of from the seminary.

The oldest claim Christians make — older than the buildings, older than the committees, older than the arguments that drove you out — is that God did not manage the world from a distance. God showed up in it. As a body. A particular body, in a particular backwater town, with a particular accent and calloused hands and a mother who worried about him. Whatever else you do with that claim, notice its shape: presence over pronouncement. God's answer to human pain was not a memo. It was proximity.

Now here's the uncomfortable part, the part that took me years and a diagnosis to learn. Most of us who grew up around religion absorbed the opposite shape. We learned that the holy way to love someone is from slightly above them. You show up with the answer. You show up with the casserole and the verse and the certainty, and underneath the kindness there's a quiet transaction: I am the well one, you are the sick one, and my presence is a form of expertise. It feels like love. It functions like distance.

I know this because I did it for years, professionally, and because I have now spent enough time on the receiving end to feel the difference in my own chest. I'm bipolar. When I finally started saying that sentence out loud, I got two kinds of visitors. The first kind came with a plan for me. They had read something, or they knew someone, or they had a strong opinion about medication, and their eyes were doing that thing where they're looking at you but scanning you, checking you against a checklist. I always felt smaller when they left.

The second kind just came. One friend sat on my porch and told me about the year his own mind broke, and he didn't tie it up with a lesson. He let it stay broken in the telling. He wasn't there as a well man visiting a sick one. He was there as one limping man sitting next to another, and I swear the porch felt different afterward — not fixed, but inhabited. Something had moved in.

That's the difference I mean. Presence that descends versus presence that joins. And the wild claim at the center of this faith — the one that survives even when the institution doesn't — is that God chose joining. Not a rescue helicopter hovering above the flood, but a man standing in the water, getting wet, getting cold, eventually going under.

If that's true, it rearranges what it means for you and me to love anybody. It means your qualification for showing up in someone's pain is not your wellness. It's your wounds. It means the most sacred thing you may ever do is sit in a kitchen with someone whose life is on fire and resist — with your whole body resist — the urge to be helpful in the way that makes you feel tall. It means the people the church accidentally trained to stay home until they're better, until they're sober, until they're sure, until the depression lifts and the doubt quiets — those people were always the ones who understood the assignment best. You can't join someone in the low place if you've never admitted you live there too.

I posted something during a manic stretch once, some version of "everything feels amazing, why is everyone telling me to slow down," and later, when the ground came back, I had to look at it. Here's what I found underneath the embarrassment: even at my most unwell, what reached me was never the person with the correct assessment of my condition. It was the person who stayed in the room with my condition. Heather didn't love me from the far side of the diagnosis. She stood inside the blast radius. That's not a strategy. That's a body, choosing proximity, paying the cost of it. If you want to know what I think God is like, I don't point at a pulpit anymore. I point at that.

So here's what I'd ask of you, if you're one of the ones outside the gate, loving God and unable to stomach the machinery. Don't wait to be credentialed. Don't wait to be well. The posture is available to you tonight: one body, moving toward another body, carrying nothing but your actual self — your history, your limp, your unfinished sentences. Sit down. Stay. Let the silence be part of the visit. You will be tempted every few minutes to reach for altitude, to say the thing that sounds like it settles something. Put it down. The tissues on the windowsill will tell you what to do.

I no longer believe the gate keeps anyone from God. I've come to suspect it mostly keeps us from each other. But if presence is the whole shape of the thing — if joining is what God actually does — then a harder question comes next, and I can't put it off much longer: what do you do with the parts of yourself you've been told disqualify you from the room? Because I brought my broken brain to that hospital bedside, and to every bedside since. And I'm going to tell you what I found out about the wound you think is the reason you can't serve. It's the tool.
