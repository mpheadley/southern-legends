Every pastor who has come to Anniston First United Methodist Church has had to learn the building.

Where the fuses are. Which door sticks in July. What the sanctuary sounds like when a thunderstorm comes in off the ridge. How to get the fellowship hall to cooperate on a Sunday morning when three hundred people need to eat breakfast at the same time. The institutional knowledge of a large historic church is enormous and unglamorous and mostly invisible until someone who has it isn't there anymore.

Vernon Thompson has it.

He is the Building Superintendent at Anniston First United Methodist — at 1431 Gurnee Avenue, on the corner of Noble Street and the city's ministerial memory. He has watched pastors arrive and orient themselves and find their footing and then leave for other appointments. He has stayed.

---

## What a Building Superintendent Knows

There is a kind of institutional knowledge that never makes it into a newsletter.

The church historian documents the building programs and the capital campaigns and the pastoral tenures. The administrative staff keeps the membership records. The senior pastor writes the annual report. Nobody writes down what Vernon Thompson knows — which means nobody who reads the records quite understands the place the way he does.

He knows who gave what. He knows which families have been in which pews since before most of the current congregation was born. He knows what the church looked like before the renovation and after. He knows what got thrown away that probably shouldn't have been. He knows where the photographs are.

He knows which pastors were good at the work and which ones were better at looking like it. He knows which crises got resolved quietly and which ones didn't resolve. He knows the Sunday mornings that felt like the church was most itself.

This is not recorded anywhere. It lives in him.

## Why This Matters for Southern Legends

Southern Legends tells stories about people who have shaped this region without necessarily being named as shapers. The Building Superintendent of a major historic church in a mid-sized Alabama city is exactly that person.

Anniston First UMC has occupied its corner of the city for well over a century. It has been through the full arc of American Methodism's complicated Southern history. The congregation has housed ministries, controversies, music programs, grief groups, Christmas toy drives, and breakfast programs for the unhoused every Sunday at eight-thirty. It has sent money to missions and hosted politicians and navigated the UMC split of the early 2020s.

Vernon Thompson has been inside all of it.
