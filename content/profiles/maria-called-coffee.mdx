The conversation goes like this: she hands you a coffee, and then she says something about the mycelium network in old-growth forests and how fungi are the internet of the woods and how everything we call dead is not actually dead, it is just doing something else now, and you realize you have been standing at the counter for twenty minutes and you have not moved because you do not want to.

That is Maria.

She works full time at Called Coffee on Noble Street in Anniston. She makes art out of what people throw away. She forages mushrooms. She makes jewelry. She has painted murals on walls around town. She is Filipina, and she is here, in Northeast Alabama, and she is one of the more quietly interesting people I have met in this town or anywhere else.

I have known her for a couple of years now. We talk about everything — mental health, psychiatry, flowers, what it means to come from somewhere far away and build a life in a place most people drive through. She asks good questions. She listens like she is actually curious, which is rarer than it sounds.

---

The trash art came first, I think, or at least it is the thing I noticed first. Trash art is the right name for it. She takes what has been discarded — materials, scraps, objects that have been decided to have no more use — and she finds a use. Not ironically. Not as commentary. As an honest belief that the thing still has something to offer.

<PullQuote>Everything we call dead is not actually dead. It is just doing something else now.</PullQuote>

There is something to say about a Filipina woman in Anniston, Alabama, building her art practice around the idea that discarded things have value. I am not going to say it too plainly. The connections are there if you want to find them. She is not performing anything. She is just making things from what gets left behind, and the things she makes are beautiful.

The mushrooms fit. Fungi are decomposers. They work on what is dead or dying and they pull it back into the cycle. A mushroom knows things about decay and renewal that most of us do not. She finds them in the woods and she finds them beautiful and she is interested in what they know. I believe her.

---

The murals are on a different scale. A mural is public. A mural says: I was here, and I wanted you to see something. She has painted them around Anniston. They are not monuments. They are evidence of a person who noticed the wall and thought it could hold something worth looking at.

The jewelry is smaller still. Objects she makes with her hands. Things you could carry. Which is also, I think, the point.

She and Brodie shut down a karaoke night recently. I was not there. I heard about it the next time I came in for coffee. That is how it goes with Called Coffee — you miss things, and then you come back and someone tells you what happened, and you wish you had been there.

She is part of Ecclesia Collective, the arts community gathering at the CAW Arts & Wellness Center on Noble Street. She is one of the people who make that room feel like something real rather than something organized.

---

She is from the Philippines. She is in Anniston. She is behind the counter most days. She is thinking about mushrooms, and murals, and what you can make from what everyone else decided was done.

Go find her. Order something. Stand at the counter a little longer than you planned.

*Maria is at Called Coffee on Noble Street, Anniston. The Ecclesia Collective gathers at the CAW Arts & Wellness Center, 1400 Noble St, Anniston. If you want to see more of her work, ask her — she will tell you.*
