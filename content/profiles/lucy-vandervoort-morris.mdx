Lucy Vandervoort Morris grew up in Anniston. She went to Vanderbilt to study neuroscience. She went to the University of South Alabama for medical school, where she met Andrew Morris, a fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon who would return to Anniston with her. She trained in Family Medicine at St. Vincent's East in Birmingham.

And then she came home.

---

## What She Came Home To

Her mother was Martha Vandervoort. Martha was a presence in Calhoun County civic life — the kind of person whose name appears in the context of fundraisers, organizations, and the structures that hold a community together without always being named.

Martha died by suicide.

Lucy has spoken about this publicly, which means it is hers to say and mine to repeat with care. She has spoken about it because she believes speaking is how healing happens — not because it is easy, but because silence is worse. "The way that I have really healed," she has said, "is by opening up and talking."

After Martha died, the community created two things in her name: the Martha Vandervoort Memorial Scholarship at the RMC Foundation, which provides $3,000 per year, renewable for four years, for students going into healthcare fields. And Martha's Hope — the Martha Vandervoort Center to End Homelessness, operated through United Way of East Central Alabama.

The community named things for her mother because her mother mattered. Lucy came home and kept building.

---

## The Practice

Lucy Vandervoort Morris practices at C.A.R.E.S. Direct Primary Care in Anniston — a direct primary care model, which means patients pay her directly rather than through insurance intermediaries. The model is designed to create a more direct relationship between physician and patient: fewer forms, more time, a doctor who knows your name before she knows your billing code.

She is also in fellowship training through the Academy of Integrative Health and Medicine, pursuing a wider theory of healing: nutrition, herbal medicine, acupuncture, yoga therapy. She is a conventionally trained family physician building a less conventional practice. That combination — Vanderbilt neuroscience, South Alabama medicine, integrative fellowship — is not an accident. It is the shape of what she believes healing actually requires.

---

## Thursday Nights at Anniston First

On the first and third Thursday of each month, at 6 p.m., Lucy Vandervoort Morris runs a free mental health support group at Anniston First United Methodist Church — at The Bridge, 1431 Gurnee Avenue. Open to anyone 18 and older experiencing symptoms of mental illness. No cost. No documentation required.

She runs it herself. She is the facilitator and the physician and, in some sense, the daughter who knows what it looks like when the support doesn't come.

She also serves on the RMC Foundation Board, where she has been active on the Special Projects Committee since 2019. She has presented scholars at the Garden Jubilee fundraiser, connecting the memorial scholarship to the families it serves. She participates in the annual Out of the Shadows Mental Health Summit in Oxford — a community conversation about mental health that has been happening at the Oxford Civic Center since 2017.

---

## The Through Line

Lucy Vandervoort Morris is doing several things at once, and they are all the same thing. She is practicing medicine in the town where she grew up. She is building a more integrative version of what medicine can be. She is running a free Thursday-night group for people who need somewhere to put the weight down. She is tending her mother's memory through scholarship and through naming.

A Vanderbilt neuroscience graduate who reads Brené Brown and is studying yoga therapy is writing a different kind of medical story than the one Alabama's medical culture usually tells. She is writing it in Anniston, which is where she came from, which seems intentional.
