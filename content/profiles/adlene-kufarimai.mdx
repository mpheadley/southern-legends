Adlene Kufarimai grew up in Zimbabwe watching United Methodist missionaries build things.

Not buildings, necessarily — though buildings too. Hospitals. Schools. The infrastructure of a life that could outlast whatever got in its way. The missionaries came from somewhere else and put down something durable, and a girl in Mutare watched and apparently filed it away.

She came to the United States in 2000 with her husband TK and their children. She started in Children's Ministry. Not because it was the most strategic entry point — because she had children, and you work with what you know. From there she built a pastoral career across North Alabama that took her, in stages, from Youth and Music Ministry to Associate Pastor to Senior Pastor to District Director of Ethnic Ministries to a conference-wide role that now oversees the connective tissue of an entire Methodist conference.

That is not a meteoric rise. That is twenty-five years of showing up.

---

## The Education She Built

Her degree in education came from Mary Mount Teachers' College in Mutare. Her Master of Divinity came from Gammon Theological Seminary in Atlanta — a historically Black institution rooted in the same Wesleyan tradition that had sent missionaries to Zimbabwe, which is its own full circle. Her Doctor of Ministry came from Beeson Divinity School at Samford University in Birmingham — a Baptist divinity school.

She earned her doctorate in Alabama at a Baptist institution while serving as a United Methodist pastor. That kind of ecumenical range is not unusual in Alabama ministry, but it is not nothing either. It means the walls of her formation were wider than any one tradition.

---

## The Work She Did in NE Alabama

Before her current role, Adlene Kufarimai served as the Cheaha District Director of Ethnic Ministries.

The Cheaha District covers Oxford, Anniston, Jacksonville, Gadsden, Talladega, and the surrounding communities of Northeast Alabama. As district director, she was responsible for the Methodist church's outreach to and with communities that the conference's historic white Methodism had often passed over — Korean, Latino, African American, and immigrant congregations in and around Calhoun County.

She was also, in that role, the person who developed young clergy. She identified emerging leaders and sent them places — conferences, trainings, conversations where they would encounter the church's larger questions about public witness. I know this because she sent me. A young clergy conference in Washington DC, for church and society. I went because she thought I should go.

I am not sure she would remember it. I am sure it changed what I understood about what ministry in a place like this could mean.

---

## What She Did Next

On January 1, 2021, Bishop Debra Wallace-Padgett appointed Adlene Kufarimai as Director of Connectional Ministries for the North Alabama Conference — the office responsible for the relationships between the conference and its local churches, and between those churches and the wider Methodist world.

It is, in a sense, the work she has always done: connecting things that belong together. Zimbabwe to Alabama. Baptist seminary to Methodist ordination. Young clergy in Anniston to conversations happening in Washington. The work changed scale. The instinct did not.

Her husband, TK Kufarimai, is also an ordained elder in the North Alabama Conference — a pastoral couple doing ministry in the same conference, a rare and demanding arrangement that has apparently sustained them for a long time.

---

## The Through Line

There is a pattern worth naming in the career of Adlene Kufarimai: she keeps working at the margin between what the church says it is and what it actually is. Ethnic Ministries is precisely that margin — the gap between the Methodist church's stated commitments to diversity and the lived experience of communities that were, for most of the conference's history, on the other side of the aisle.

She grew up watching people build things in Zimbabwe. She came to Alabama and built things here. The institutions are different. The instinct is identical.
