Heather Headley did not set out to run a flower farm.

She set out to do the flowers for her mother's event, or her neighbor's wedding, or the church seasonal arrangement that nobody else seemed interested in doing well. She had a good eye and a willingness to figure things out, and at some point those two qualities turned into a business.

The farm came later. A plot of land outside Anniston — not large, but enough — where she started growing what she needed instead of buying it wholesale from a supplier in Atlanta or Birmingham. Growing your own changes the math: the stems are fresher, the varieties are different from what everyone else is using, and you know exactly when something was cut.

It also changes the work. A supply chain is someone else's problem. A flower farm is yours. The seasons are not abstract market conditions; they are what is blooming in your field on a given Tuesday morning when a bride texts you about centerpieces.

---

## Heather Florals

The business is Heather Florals — floral design, event work, weddings, pop-up arrangements for markets and local events. The Bloom Bar is the retail expression of it: a flower bar concept where people can come in and build their own arrangements, or pick up what's seasonal and local.

She supplies other florists in the area. When someone is short on a particular stem or a particular week gets too heavy, Heather Florals is the call. That matters in a place like Northeast Alabama, where the floral industry has a specific geography of supply and relationship.

Her work leans seasonal and unstructured — arrangements that look like they came from the garden rather than a warehouse. That is not an accident. It is what growing your own teaches you: what is possible right now, in this season, on this piece of land.

---

## The Homestead Logic

There is a logic to growing things that the flower business made visible. You put something in the ground. You tend it. You cut it at the right moment. Then you give it to someone for the most important day of their year.

The window between cutting and giving is short. That is the whole business. Everything else — the farm, the studio, the Bloom Bar, the market presence, the supplier relationships — is infrastructure for getting that window right.

Heather Headley has spent a decade figuring out the infrastructure.
