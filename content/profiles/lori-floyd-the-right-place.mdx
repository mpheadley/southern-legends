Most directors run an organization from a desk. Lori Floyd runs one from the tree line.

Ask the people who work with her and the same picture keeps coming back: Floyd in the woods off some Anniston road, or on her knees in the dirt, checking under a house where a person has made a place to sleep. She does not wait for the people she serves to find the office and fill out a form. She goes to where the address runs out, and she keeps going.

That instinct is the whole story of The Right Place, the Anniston nonprofit she directs. It is a 501(c)(3) that helps homeless and low-income neighbors in Calhoun County find safe, decent, affordable housing, and then does the harder second thing, which is helping them stay in it. Their rule of thumb is that rent should never eat more than thirty percent of a person's income. Their real work is everything that gets in the way of that number.

---

## The clinic at nineteen

Floyd is from Jacksonville, just up the road. She did not set out to do any of this. She was headed for music education. Then, at nineteen, she took a summer job at an HIV clinic, and the summer job rearranged her life.

"That experience led me in a completely different direction," she has said. "I learned a lot of things about nonprofits, including the compassion involved in working with all different kinds of people."

She never went back to the other plan. What followed was decades of work with the people the rest of the system tends to lose track of: people living with HIV, people fighting addiction, people carrying mental illness with nowhere steady to carry it. Her motto is four words she actually lives by. "Be humble and kind." Her advice to anyone coming up behind her is just as plain. "Take the time to learn from all walks of life."

## More than a roof

The Right Place is a low-barrier organization, which is a technical way of saying they do not make you earn your dignity first. There is no clock running out on you. The help is comprehensive on purpose. They will sit with you to get a driver's license back. They connect people to mental health support. They offer rental assistance. Floyd herself leads Mental Health First Aid training for other people and organizations across the community, so the net gets wider than just her own hands.

And then there are the small, human things that tell you what kind of place it is. A room with computers, so a person can fill out an application or look for work. A coat closet. A shoe closet. Art teachers who come in on a regular basis to sit with people and make something. None of that shows up in a mission statement. All of it shows up in a life.

## She is not doing it alone

Anniston has a quiet web of people who spend themselves on the same problem, and Floyd works inside it, not above it. Interfaith Ministries of Calhoun County. United Way of East Central Alabama. Martha's Hope, the low-barrier shelter that finally opened its doors. The people who kept a warming station running on the coldest nights. Different doors, same work: getting a homeless neighbor a bed, a meal, a next step. Name them, because a legend in a place like this is rarely one person. It is a handful of people who refuse, year after year, to quit on the same folks. Floyd is one of the ones who refuses.

---

## Why she counts as a legend

Southern Legends is not a list of the famous. It is a record of the people who stayed and did the work where they were from. Lori Floyd took a summer job at nineteen and let it point her at the hardest, least glamorous corner of her own county, and she has not looked away from it since.

She is not a hero in the Hollywood sense, and she would be the first to wave that word off. She is something more useful. She is a witness who shows up, kneels down, and keeps a coat closet stocked for the next person the rest of us did not see.
