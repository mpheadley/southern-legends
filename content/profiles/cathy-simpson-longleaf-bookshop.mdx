Anniston did not have an independent bookshop.

Then Cathy Simpson and her daughter Sarah opened Longleaf Bookshop and Bindery on Noble Street, and now it does.

That is how these things work in small cities. Someone decides the thing is worth doing, finds a space that can hold it, and opens. The gap that seemed permanent turns out to have been waiting for someone stubborn enough to fill it.

Longleaf is both a bookshop and a bindery — which is unusual. The bookbinding side is not an afterthought; it is part of what makes the place specific. Binding is the craft of making a book into a physical object you can hold and return to. In a moment when most reading is digital and most purchases are from a warehouse in New Jersey, there is something deliberate about a shop that also makes the thing it sells.

---

## Noble Street

Noble Street is in the middle of a long, slow conversation about what it wants to be. The buildings are old and some of them are beautiful. The blocks have a lot of empty storefronts still, and a few places that have decided to be somewhere.

Longleaf is one of the places that decided.

Cathy Simpson runs the shop with her daughter Sarah. The mother-daughter business is its own kind of story — a particular form of inheritance, the thing that gets passed down through proximity and learning and shared stubbornness about what is worth doing.

---

## What a Bookshop Does

A bookshop does more than sell books. It establishes a neighborhood's relationship to reading as a public act. It is a place where you can browse without buying, where you can find something you didn't know you were looking for, where the selection reflects someone's judgment about what is worth reading.

Longleaf Bookshop and Bindery does all of that. On Noble Street, in Anniston, which is exactly the right place for it.
