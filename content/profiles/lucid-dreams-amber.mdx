There is a particular kind of trust a tattoo requires. You sit down in a chair, hand your arm or your back or your ribs to someone, and you let them put on you something that does not come off. Amber Owens has never treated that lightly. "Getting a tattoo, it's permanent," she says. "You're having a life-to-life connection with somebody else."

She built Lucid Dreams around that idea. And she built it in the one place that spent a hundred years making sure a place like it couldn't exist.

## The name

Start with the name, because the name is the whole philosophy in two words. "Lucid dream is when you are in control of your dream," Amber says. "That's not only our mission, but how we want our clients to feel when it comes to making their own decisions for their body." A tattoo, done right, is not something that happens to you. It's something you author.

The name is older than the shop. As a kid, Amber had vivid nightmares — night terrors, sleep paralysis — and the way she learned to cope was by teaching herself to lucid dream, to take the wheel inside her own head. "It was like a promise to myself when I was a kid," she says. "If I open a business one day, I'm gonna call it Lucid Dreams."

## Leaving, and coming back on purpose

Amber is from here — Oxford, Anniston, the county. She left at twenty, moved to Nevada, and worked about a year in a shop out there before the owner decided to move on and offered her his lease. She opened her own studio at twenty-one and ran it, for a good year, alone in a 900-square-foot room.

Then something shifted in how she thought about the work. "I can make money with it, yes," she says. "But this is a very personal experience." She started asking what her purpose actually was — and realized she'd never make the impact she wanted in Nevada, where she was on the coattails of an art scene that was already somebody else's home. A trip to Asheville, North Carolina — a downtown not unlike Anniston's, but thriving and artistically alive — settled it. She wanted to come home and help Anniston reach that.

So she did. "Since I changed my view of things and made it more mission driven, rather than just economically driven," she says, "the people that came into my orbit were also like-minded in that way." The proof is in who stayed: "Other than two people, we've had 100% retention." Her artists don't just pass through.

## The hundred-year fight

Here is the part most people don't know. Anniston has, in Amber's words, "some really interesting tattoo karma."

Around the turn of the last century, a tattooer named Percy Waters — born in Anniston in 1880 — tattooed the daughter of one of the city's founding families and was effectively run out of town. In the fallout, tattoo studios were zoned out of Anniston proper. Waters went to Detroit and became one of the forefathers of the American tattoo trade: he patented tattoo machines still referenced today and helped build the modern tattoo-supply industry, all while calling himself, humbly, "just another good tattooer." He came home to Anniston in his later years and ran his supply company here until he died.

Amber didn't know any of this when she started — until she learned that the coil machine she learned to tattoo on was a Percy Waters prototype. A woman leaving Anniston to master a trade and coming back to plant it downtown, a century after a man did the same in reverse: it's the kind of loop that makes a Southern legend.

And she had to fight the same old ordinance to do it. "We had to advocate for ourselves for over a year and a half to be able to get into the downtown area," she says — weekly town-hall meetings, an older generation turning out "in droves to speak against us," constant conversations with council members. She also wrote the owners of her building a letter every single month — nobody required it — asking them to keep the contract, laying out her mission, staying transparent. It paid off. The studio has held the building about two and a half years and been open in the space about two. And the direct neighbors who fought her hardest? "A couple of weeks ago, they actually gave me a very sincere apology."

She didn't want to be the only one, either. She pushed for the ordinance to change so that *other* studios could follow her downtown. "I'd rather there be some camaraderie in the community," she says, "rather than any hostility or otherness."

## Dreamfest

Every year, Lucid Dreams throws a free festival — Summer Dreamfest, out at the Longleaf Event Center at the [Anniston Museums and Gardens](/profiles/anniston-museums-gardens): live music, local artisans, vintage goods, food trucks, the whole county invited, no cover. It returns as **Dreamfest: Solar Faire** on Saturday, July 18, 2026.

For Amber, Dreamfest is a discipline as much as an event. She describes the first one as a decision to show up with gratitude no matter what — "no matter what, I'm going to have immense gratitude for today. Period." She got it, and the day, she says, was perfect. Every year since is a reminder to keep showing up that way. A tattoo shop does not have to give the town a free festival. She does it because the shop was never only about ink.

## The chair, and the door

Lucid Dreams is a queer- and women-owned studio, ten artists deep, by appointment, on West 10th Street. It carries 237 reviews on Facebook at 94% recommend and more than a thousand posts of finished work on Instagram — the quiet, daily proof of a room people trust.

But the truest thing about it is the philosophy folded into the name. A safe chair, where you stay in control of your own dream. Amber Owens left Anniston to learn how to build one, came home on purpose, and then argued the city into letting her put it exactly where it belongs — downtown, doors open, a century of "no" finally answered.
