Aaron Norris grew up in Andalusia, Alabama — the Wiregrass, far south, as different from the Appalachian foothills of Calhoun County as Alabama gets while still being Alabama.

He went to Auburn for his bachelor's degree. Then stayed for a Master of Community Planning. Then stayed again for a second master's. Three degrees from the same institution is not indecision — it is the kind of focused depth that comes from knowing what you want to do and learning everything it requires.

He is AICP-certified: the American Institute of Certified Planners, a national professional credential that requires documented experience and a written examination. He served in the Marine Corps Reserve during his time at Auburn. He is 33 years old.

---

## Alabama Before Massachusetts

After Auburn, Norris went to the Alabama Department of Commerce — not to Montgomery in the abstract, but to the project-level work of rural economic development across forty Alabama counties. This is a particular kind of education: traveling to the actual places, working on actual sites, understanding how industrial recruitment and small-business support work when the place you're working in has a struggling tax base and a lot of square footage in old industrial buildings.

Forty rural Alabama counties is a good foundation for understanding what places need and what they can hold.

---

## Three Years in Brookline

Then he went to Brookline, Massachusetts.

Brookline is a wealthy, progressive, densely built suburb adjacent to Boston — one of the places in America where planners have the most tools, the most community engagement, and the most complicated politics. It is not rural Alabama. It is the opposite of rural Alabama in almost every measurable dimension.

Norris worked there as Senior Economic Development and Long-Term Planner. His work included ARPA projects, small-business support programs, community engagement initiatives, and public art programs that received national attention. He was, by any measure, doing sophisticated urban planning in a sophisticated urban environment.

His partner had gone north for work at MIT and then moved into Boston tech. That is what brought him there. What brought him back was Anniston.

---

## November 2025

He arrived as Director of Economic Development and City Planning on November 14, 2025.

He succeeded longtime director Toby Bennington. He walked into a city with a complicated economic history, a downtown corridor with more vacant storefronts than anyone wants to count, and a community that has been told for decades that it is on the verge of something.

His first public statement was: "I'm really kind of trying to be a sponge right now and soak up everything because the community members are what makes Anniston Anniston."

That is the right answer. It is also not nothing — a planner with three Auburn degrees and three years in Brookline who shows up saying he wants to listen is making a choice about posture. Anniston has had enough people arrive with plans. A planner who arrives saying he wants to absorb the place first is a particular kind of person.

---

## The Diaspora Return

Norris is not from Calhoun County. He is from the Wiregrass. But his path — Alabama education, Alabama government work, north for a season, return to Alabama — is a version of the diaspora story this region knows well. He did not come back to his hometown. He came to a city that needed what he had learned.

What he does with that education and that posture in Anniston will take years to know. He has been here since November. He says he is a sponge.
