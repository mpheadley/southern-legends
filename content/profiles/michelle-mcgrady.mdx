I walked into Old Noble on a Saturday looking for nothing in particular, and I stopped in front of a painting of a building I have driven past a thousand times. It was one of those downtown Anniston storefronts with the tall front windows and the brick worn soft at the corners. I knew the building. I did not know I had ever really looked at it until somebody painted it and hung it on a wall where I had to stop.

The painter is Michelle McGrady. Her work is on the walls at Old Noble and around the corner at Nunnally's Noble Frame & Gallery, both on Noble Street. Whimsical, she calls it, and that is the right word. The colors run warmer and brighter than the real thing. The lines lean a little. The buildings look like they are remembering themselves rather than posing for a photograph.

Before she was a painter she was a teacher. Nearly thirty years of third grade in the Anniston City School System. That is a long time to stand in one town's classrooms. Long enough to teach the children of children you taught. Long enough to watch the downtown storefronts change hands and change again. She spent those years handing kids their letters and their numbers, and then she retired and turned full time to painting the buildings where all of it happened.

<PullQuote>She spent thirty years teaching Anniston's children, and now she paints the town they grew up in.</PullQuote>

There is something honest about that order of things. She did not paint the buildings and then teach in them. She taught first. She put in the decades. The painting is what came after, and it carries all of it. A person who has spent thirty years in the same town does not paint it as a stranger passing through with a good camera. She paints it as somebody who knows which corner floods when it rains and which diner used to be a hardware store.

## What she paints

Historic buildings, mostly. Downtown Anniston and the little towns near it. Storefronts, church fronts, the kind of old Northeast Alabama architecture that most of us have quit seeing because it is always there. That is the thing about a building you pass every day. It goes invisible. It becomes scenery. What she does is put it back in front of you at an angle where you have to notice it again.

Her art brings people together, starts conversations, and stirs something in whoever stops to look. I believe that, because I watched it happen to me in the middle of a shop I had walked into for no reason at all. Somebody stopped in front of the same painting a minute after I did and said the name of the building out loud like they were greeting an old friend. That is not nothing. In a town where a lot of the old places have come down or emptied out, a painting that makes two strangers say the same building's name is doing quiet, real work.

You can find her at Anniston's downtown market too, the same market where I have spent a lot of Saturday mornings helping Heather sell flowers. The market is the right place for her. It is where the town shows up as itself, and her paintings belong to that same crowd of people who still bother to come downtown and stand around and talk.

## The chapter after

The Anniston Star ran a piece on her once with a line about painting a new chapter. I keep coming back to that. A new chapter is not the same as a new life. She did not leave Anniston to become somebody else somewhere else. She stayed. She changed what she does with her days, and the subject stayed exactly the same. The town. The buildings. The place she gave three decades to.

<PullQuote>A painting that makes two strangers say the same building's name is doing quiet, real work.</PullQuote>

I like that she is not chasing anything grand with it. She is not painting to be discovered. She is painting the courthouse and the storefronts and the old church fronts because she looked at them for thirty years and now she has time to look harder. There is a kind of faithfulness in that. You keep the thing you were given. You hand it back to people a little brighter than you found it.

That is most of what any of us can do with a place. Notice it. Keep it. Hand it on. Michelle McGrady spent her working life handing Anniston's children their alphabet, and now she is handing the whole town its own buildings back, painted warm, hung on Noble Street, waiting for somebody to walk in on a Saturday and stop.

I stopped. I will be back.

If you want to see the same thing I saw, her paintings of Piedmont's old theater district are worth the drive, and while you are thinking about the arts in this corner of Alabama, read about [Jason Wright and the Piedmont theater](/journal/jason-wright-piedmont-theater) too. Same instinct, different hands. People who love a place enough to keep it in front of the rest of us.
