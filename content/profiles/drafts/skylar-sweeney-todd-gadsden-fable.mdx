I played Sweeney Todd once, at twenty-three, on a college stage with a rotating set and a steampunk coat, before I understood a word of what I was singing. Almost two decades later I walked into a rehearsal hall in Gadsden, twenty-five minutes before the night's rehearsal, and watched a cast half my memory and twice my nerve build the thing again from scratch.

Seventy-four, maybe seventy-five people showed up to audition. Nobody in the room could land on the exact number, which tells you something. I was one of them. I did not get cast, and I stand by what I said that night anyway: you could not cram so much talent into a room here in Gadsden. Somebody did.

"I ended up overcasting it," Skylar admitted. "My limit was twenty-five, and after I casted what I wanted, it was like forty-eight." He could not bring himself to send that much voice home. {/* VERIFY: attribution — diarization scrambles this exchange on both mics; casting call reads as the director's, but confirm against audio */}

That is the first thing you should know about this production. It did not have to be this big. Somebody decided it would be.

## The man who wanted scale

Skylar is the director, though he is quick to hand the credit sideways. When I told him the set was fantastic — and it is, a decaying grandeur that reads like *Gone with the Wind* left out in the weather — he corrected me before I finished the sentence. "It's better to say I helped. Michael took the brunt of that. Michael and Joe. I've been at work a lot." The Joe is Joe Fuselli, his assistant director.

But the vision is his, and the vision is not small. There is a classic way to stage *Sweeney Todd*: a spare rectangular box that rotates. Skylar knows that box. He was in this show fifteen years ago, in this same town, playing Pirelli on a turning stage. He could have reached for the safe version.

"When I decided to take the reins, I just wanted scale," he told me. "I wanted something huge and impactful, because I knew I had the talent that was huge and impactful." He said the word *standard* the way a man says a thing he is a little afraid to want out loud. Not a standard he was setting, he insisted — one he was reaching back toward, the standard of the big shows this community used to mount. *Les Misérables.* The ones you remember for years.

Sondheim, he thinks, wrote his most intricate, busiest, biggest piece here, and Skylar has gone looking in it. He will tell you the *Dies Irae* — the old Latin chant for the day of wrath — runs under the whole score as a motif, and that Mrs. Lovett's first line might be that chant in reverse. "I might be wrong," he said, and then went home that night to listen and check. There are layers in every song you could spend hours pulling apart and still find something new. He directs to those motifs, even in the blocking, so the dread gets into you underneath the words. "Even if people don't catch that right off, it still is something that subconsciously layers up and builds."

He gave the whole production a private theme and worked it into the costumes, the choreography, the set. He calls it *beauty decaying.* Every character on that stage was beautiful once, or is beautiful still, and in every one of them the decay has set in — some by their choices, some by their circumstances. Sweeney, obviously. But also Joanna, who stands for innocence, rotting a little inside her cage. "You could apply that theme to almost any situation," he said, "and still be able to find how it is true for each character."

I have not stopped thinking about it since. It is true for the characters. It is also true for the rest of us in the room.

## The dark and hungry god

The reason I wanted to write about this show is not the razor. It is a question Skylar has carried for fifteen years and only recently answered for himself.

*Sweeney Todd* says, in one of its darkest lines, that its barber *serves a dark and a vengeful god,* and later, that he is *a dark and hungry god* himself. When Skylar first stood inside this show as a younger man, the line snagged him. "I was like, which god are they talking about? Maybe it's just some named false god." It is never named. So he sat with it, and the answer he came to is the whole sermon.

"I think the dark and vengeful god he is serving is himself," Skylar said. "He chooses self over everything else."

He is careful about the difference between vengeance and revenge. Sweeney is not, in the end, avenging anyone but Sweeney, Skylar argues — and the proof is the daughter. "He even says goodbye to his daughter without even making plans to reconcile with her after he kills the judge. There's none of that. It's all just, *goodbye, Joanna, and these are my plans.* It's very self-serving. This is what I want. This is what I need to get my own satisfaction." Then he said the sentence that made me put my pen down. "I think there's parallels to that a lot with what we do in our own actions, in our own lives. Obviously maybe not to that extreme."

This is a man of faith directing one of the most violent things ever set to music, and he is not flinching and he is not sanitizing. On the phone weeks earlier he had told me about a prayer he keeps returning to, one he once heard prayed over a production like this: *Lord, I pray you somehow get glory out of all this debauchery.* {/* VERIFY: prayer wording is from the phone call, not on tape — the tape only confirms the glory-out-of-debauchery idea when Matt raises it and Skylar agrees */}

His theology of the thing is almost Augustine. "I don't think darkness necessarily exists," he said. "Darkness is just the absence of light. You can't bring darkness into a room. You can only bring light into a room. And if you are just left with darkness, you're left to your own devices and your own aimless decisions that end up tearing your soul apart." Even in the Christian framework, he added, there is no darkness in God at all — so the dark, staged honestly, is not the enemy of the light. It is the outline that lets you see it. {/* VERIFY: attribution on the "Christian framework / no darkness" line — the two mics disagree on the speaker at this crosstalk moment */}

He has a quote he lives by, from the poet Caesar A. Cruz, pinned to his own profile: *Art should comfort the disturbed and disturb the comfortable.* "No matter where you are in your life," he said, "there's something from the arts that should speak to you and move you from the place where you are to somewhere else." That is the goal, on purpose, of this production.

## The man I did not beat

Jason Wright is playing Sweeney.

I should tell you plainly that I auditioned for this show, that I have known Jason for twenty years, and that watching him step into a role I once carried is a complicated and mostly good thing to feel. I have [written elsewhere](/journal/jason-got-sweeney) about the envy and the relief and the grace of it, so I will not relitigate my own small drama here. I will only say that the room made the right call, and that I know it because of how Jason talks about the part.

He struggled with "Epiphany," Sweeney's great unraveling aria — not the notes, the meaning. "I was having such a hard time really getting into it, and I didn't want to do it just like how I felt like everyone would possibly do it." So he went back to the words, and something opened. "This is like a salvation moment for him. He's kind of lost his humanity. He's had everything stripped from him and taken away. And he sort of unifies with something divine that's past human — to me, like a god of vengeance. And so then he says, *I will have salvation, and the work waits.*"

"I don't get to be bad very often," he admitted. He told his piano student about the show that afternoon, and when she asked whether it was really okay for her to come see it, he did not hesitate. No — you *should.* "That's the wonderful thing about art. If you censor everything, you don't get an opportunity to see all the colors of the human experience. And we are human. We're going to experience all of them."

Jason teaches theater, and half the young people in this cast came up under him. Dax, nineteen, playing Toby; Gracie as Joanna; JoJo, Grayson, Jake — kids he first put on a stage now standing on one beside him. I asked what that was like and he gave me the truest answer anybody gave me all night.

"It's just like teaching with my students. Every time I get to be in a show with them, it kind of reminds me of why I do this." He turned the phrase over, finding the better version of it. "You get gifted more people to love." {/* VERIFY: attribution — diarization tags this tail-of-night exchange inconsistently; content matches Jason (the teacher of JoJo/Grayson/Jake/Dax) but confirm against audio */}

He says he feels a little rusty. He is not rusty. He is home.

## The mouthpiece and the maestro

If Jason's Sweeney is stoic and interior, Amanda's Mrs. Lovett does all the talking — and Amanda knows it. She and Skylar were both in that production fifteen years ago; she was ensemble then, and she is stepping now into a role she once watched somebody else play.

"The show is called *Sweeney Todd*, but Mrs. Lovett does all of the talking. She's the mouthpiece to his stoicism." It's really the Mrs. Lovett show, she said, starring Sweeney. "She's zero to a hundred all the time." The hard part is not the avalanche of words, though there is that. It is the woman underneath them, constantly pulling him back with one hand and offering him more with the other. "She wants to do anything to please him. She'll do anything to make him hers. You have to be a little off-kilter in the first place to be willing to cross certain lines." Amanda has been chasing the nuance in a character who never slows down. "She's always got a plan. She's always thinking ahead — ten, twenty years ahead. She knows how to not only play a situation but how to emotionally manipulate people." An enabler, in the end. The one who hands the monster his best idea.

Then there is Silas, the music director, conducting Sondheim for the first time and clearly a little in love with the assignment. He hears the score as a set of arguments carried by the soloists — "Sweeney kind of represents selfishness, and Joanna represents purity, and Antony represents hope" — with the ensemble used sparingly to hold those ideas up. The opposite, he pointed out, of the last big show he led, *Frozen*, where the crowd pushes the story forward and the leads punctuate it.

He is honest about how strange the music is. "It's part of that expressionism-type period, where you have nonsensical things you're trying to make sense of, and sometimes the best thing you can do is not try to make sense of it and just do it. Because that's part of what the story is trying to do. Bad things happen. Sometimes there's bad things happen in the music, and you've just got to keep moving." Strip away what is harsh to some ears, Silas said, and what is left is a very lush, very thick, contrasting score. "It's humbling to be able to lead it."

## Make room for the dark

Near the end, standing in a hall that smelled like sawdust and fresh paint, minutes from places, we got honest about why any of us make a thing like this.

Amanda said the oldest true thing about light: you cannot know it without the dark to walk away from. "If it was all one or the other, there would be no contrast. You would never know your way to the light, because there's nothing to walk away from." The whole show, she said, is a mirror and a warning. "Who hasn't told a lie before? Who hasn't manipulated someone before? You see little pieces of yourself in these characters — and you also say, *oh, but that's wrong.* It's a morality tale. It's a cautionary tale." Skylar quoted her on it later without noticing he was doing it. {/* VERIFY: attribution — mic 1 tags this speech as Jason, mic 2 as Amanda; Skylar's on-tape "like Amanda said, this is a cautionary tale" tips it to Amanda. Confirm against audio. */}

I gave them the phrase I could not shake. *Hurt people hurt people.* Skylar had heard it that very day, he said. I offered the one I hold onto against it — that the things that happen to you, even the ones with no reason attached, ultimately find their place in your life. He liked that better. So do I. It is the sentence Sweeney could never learn to say, and it is the reason the show still works as a warning instead of a wallow.

They are making room for the dark on that stage in Gadsden — a room full of beautiful, decaying people telling the truth about a man who chose himself and called it a god. When the lights come up, some of us will see the razor. A few of us, if Skylar has done what he set out to do, will see the mirror.

I played this part once, before I understood it. These folks understand it. Go watch them.
