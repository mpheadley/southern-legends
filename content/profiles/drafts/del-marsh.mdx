Del Marsh was not from here.

He was born September 2, 1956, in Wheeling, West Virginia. His family moved to Alabama when he was five years old, and he grew up in East Lake, on the east side of Birmingham, watching his father sell cars. That is a particular kind of Alabama childhood: a salesman's son, learning early that the whole thing runs on whether a person trusts you enough to sign. He went to Auburn. And then, at some point, he did the thing that made him one of us: he came to Calhoun County, and he built something, and he stayed.

The something was Aerospace Coatings International, an FAA-certified repair station he founded in Oxford in the 1990s. It refurbished worn and broken airplane parts, the unglamorous, exacting work of making a component airworthy again, and it grew to employ around 130 people. In a county that has spent forty years learning what it means to lose an Army depot's worth of jobs, a man who builds 130 of them out of nothing is a figure of consequence before he ever runs for anything. He sold the company to the Utah-based Wencor Group in 2011, a few months after taking the Senate gavel.

He ran for something. In 1998 he won the District 12 seat in the Alabama State Senate. He held it for twenty-four years.

<PullQuote>
For a decade, the most powerful person in Alabama state government who wasn't the governor kept his home, his base, and his phone number in Calhoun County.
</PullQuote>

## The Gavel

In November 2010, Alabama Republicans took control of the state legislature for the first time since Reconstruction. It was not a small thing. It was the political event of a generation in this state: the end of a Democratic majority that had held, in one form or another, for a hundred and thirty years.

The man the new majority put in charge of the Senate was Del Marsh. Elected President Pro Tempore on December 8, 2010, he became the presiding officer of the upper chamber, and in practice one of the two or three people who decided what Alabama did and did not do. He held the post for a decade, into 2021, and served a stretch as Acting President of the Senate besides.

What he did with it depended on where you sat. He was the chief architect of the Republican governing project in the Senate: he pushed through the 2013 Alabama Accountability Act, which created tax-credit scholarships for students to leave failing public schools. He drove the 2015 law that made charter schools legal in Alabama for the first time, and he kept coming back to it. As late as 2022 he was still filing bills to let public money follow students into charter schools. He sponsored the 2013 legislation that folded twelve separate state law-enforcement agencies into the single Alabama Law Enforcement Agency, ALEA, which took effect on the first day of 2015.

To his supporters, that is a record: education reform, consolidated law enforcement, a Republican governing majority built and held. To his critics, and there were many, especially among public-school teachers and their union, the charter and accountability fights were an assault on public education dressed up as choice. Both things were said out loud, for years, in Montgomery and in the letters pages of the Anniston Star.

## The Gambling Fight

The last big thing he tried, he did not get.

In 2021, Marsh put his name on the most ambitious gambling bill Alabama had seen in a generation: SB214, a constitutional amendment that would have created a state lottery, licensed as many as ten casinos, stood up an Alabama Gaming Commission, and legalized sports betting. It was a genuinely large swing at a question the state had ducked for decades: Alabama is one of the last states without a lottery, and every year the money keeps crossing the line into Tennessee, Georgia, Florida, and Mississippi.

The bill passed the Senate 19 to 13. It failed anyway. A constitutional amendment needs 21 votes, and he was two short.

Along the way, it got ugly. In March 2021, a coalition of plaintiffs (Age With Dignity Inc. and two Greene County organizations) sued Marsh, alleging that his gambling plan was a "pay to play" scheme that would hand an illegal monopoly to the Poarch Band of Creek Indians, and accusing him of soliciting bribes.

<PullQuote>
Marsh denied all of it. His legislative power, he said, was not for sale. He called the lawsuit a lie.
</PullQuote>

It is worth being careful here, and being fair. A lawsuit is an allegation, not a finding. Marsh was not convicted of anything; he denied the claims flatly and publicly. And this was not the first time a suit had come at him for something done under the dome. In 2013, in *Pettway v. Del Marsh*, the Alabama Supreme Court held that Marsh and other legislators were immune from suit for acts inside "the sphere of legitimate legislative activity," and ordered the case dismissed. Powerful men in Montgomery get sued. It comes with the gavel. But the 2021 suit landed on the biggest bill of his career, at the end of it, and it is part of the record.

## What He Leaves

He did not run again. His long service in the Alabama Senate ended in November 2022, and the seat he had held since the Clinton administration passed to a new generation of Calhoun County Republicans fighting it out in a primary runoff.

There is a version of this story that is a campaign brochure, and a version that is an indictment, and neither one is true. The true one is more interesting and more Alabama than either.

A car salesman's son from West Virginia landed in Anniston, built a company that made a living for a hundred and thirty of his neighbors, and turned that into a quarter-century in the Senate and ten years holding its gavel, and he did it all without ever pretending Montgomery was home. He reshaped how Alabama funds its schools and organizes its police. He came up two votes short of finally answering the lottery question. And he left with a big unproven allegation still attached to his last big fight.

That is not a saint and it is not a villain. That is power, the real, local, complicated kind, and for a while it ran out of a shop in Calhoun County.
