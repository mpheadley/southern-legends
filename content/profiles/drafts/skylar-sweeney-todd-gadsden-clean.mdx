There is a safe way to stage *Sweeney Todd*. A spare box that rotates. A cast you can count on one hand of principals. Skylar Wheat knew the safe way — he was in this show fifteen years ago, in this same town, playing Pirelli on a turning stage. He did not choose it.

"When I decided to take the reins, I just wanted scale," Wheat says. "I wanted something huge and impactful, because I knew I had the talent that was huge and impactful."

He had the talent. Seventy-four or seventy-five people turned out to audition — a number the company still repeats with a little disbelief. The plan was to cast around twenty-five. They ended up closer to fifty, because no one could bear to send that much voice home.

Wheat talks about a *standard* the way a person talks about a thing they are almost afraid to want out loud. Not one he is setting, he insists — one he is reaching back toward, the standard of the big shows this community used to mount. The *Les Misérables* years. The ones people remember. He is quick to name the people carrying it with him — Michael and Joe on the build, his assistant director Joe Fuselli among them — and a new partnership with Gadsden State that the company is plainly proud of. {/* VERIFY: exact nature of the Gadsden State partnership — get specifics from Skylar before publish. */}

## Beauty, decaying

The set reads like grandeur left out in the weather, and that is on purpose. Wheat gave the whole production a private theme and worked it into the costumes, the choreography, the design. He calls it *beauty decaying.* Every character on the stage was beautiful once, or is beautiful still, and in each of them the decay has set in — some by their choices, some by their circumstances. Sweeney, obviously. But also Joanna, who stands for innocence, rotting a little inside her cage. "You could apply that to almost any situation," Wheat says, "and still find how it's true for each character."

He has gone looking in the score, too. He will tell you the *Dies Irae* — the old Latin chant for the day of wrath — runs under the whole thing as a motif, and that Mrs. Lovett's first line may be that chant played backward. "I might be wrong," he says, and then admits he planned to go home that night and check. He directs to those motifs even in the blocking, so the dread settles in underneath the words. "Even if people don't catch it right off, it still layers up and builds."

## Reading the barber

Sondheim's barber sings, in one of the show's darkest lines, that he serves "a dark and a vengeful god." As a younger man in an earlier production, Wheat wondered which god the song meant — some named villain, maybe. He decided, in the end, that there is no other god in it.

"The dark and vengeful god he is serving is himself," Wheat says. "He chooses self over everything else." He is careful about the difference between vengeance and revenge: Sweeney never makes a plan to reconcile with the daughter he could still save. "It's all just, *goodbye, Joanna, and these are my plans.* It's very self-serving." Then the reading that makes the show more than a horror story: "There's parallels to that with what we do in our own lives. Maybe not to that extreme."

It is a director's interpretation, and it drives the staging. Wheat does not flinch at the violence or sand it down. "I don't think darkness necessarily exists," he says. "Darkness is just the absence of light. You can't bring darkness into a room. You can only bring light. If you're left with darkness, you're left to your own aimless decisions — the ones that end up tearing your soul apart." The dark, staged honestly, is the outline that lets an audience see everything else.

He keeps a line from the poet Caesar A. Cruz close: *Art should comfort the disturbed and disturb the comfortable.* That is the aim here, on purpose.

## The company

Jason Wright plays Sweeney. A longtime theater teacher, Wright struggled at first with "Epiphany," the barber's great unraveling — not the notes, the meaning. He went back to the words. "It's like a salvation moment for him. He's lost his humanity, everything's been stripped away, and he unifies with something divine that's past human." He grins at the darkness of the part. "I don't get to be bad very often." Several of the young actors around him came up under his teaching — Dax, nineteen, as Toby; Gracie as Joanna; JoJo, Grayson, Jake. Sharing a stage with former students, he says, "reminds me why I do this. You get gifted more people to love."

Amanda plays Mrs. Lovett, and knows the paradox of the part. "The show is called *Sweeney Todd*, but Mrs. Lovett does all the talking. She's the mouthpiece to his stoicism." The hard work, she says, is the woman underneath the avalanche of words — always planning, always ten or twenty years ahead, pulling him back with one hand and offering him more with the other. "She knows how to emotionally manipulate people." The enabler, in the end. The one who hands the monster his best idea.

Music director Silas is conducting Sondheim for the first time and is plainly a little in love with the assignment. He hears the score as a set of arguments carried by the soloists — Sweeney for selfishness, Joanna for purity, Antony for hope — with the ensemble used sparingly to hold those ideas up. It is strange, expressionist music, he says, full of things you stop trying to make sense of and simply play. Strip away what is harsh to some ears and what remains is lush and thick and full of contrast. "It's humbling to be able to lead it."

They are making room for the dark on a stage in Gadsden — a company of beautiful, decaying people telling the truth about a man who chose himself and called it a god. Some in the audience will see the razor. If Wheat has done what he set out to do, a few will see the mirror.
