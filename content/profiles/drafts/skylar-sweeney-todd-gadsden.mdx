I played Sweeney Todd once, at twenty-three, on a college stage with a rotating set and a steampunk coat, before I understood a word of what I was singing. Almost two decades later I walked into a rehearsal hall in Gadsden, a room I had auditioned for and not been cast in the way I once was, and watched a cast half my memory and twice my nerve build the thing again from scratch.

There were seventy-five people at those auditions. The music director told me that himself, still a little stunned by it. "You could not cram so much talent into a room here in Gadsden," he said, and then admitted he had blown past his own cap — he meant to cast twenty-five and ended up closer to fifty, because he could not bring himself to send that much voice home.

That is the first thing you should know about this production. It did not have to be this big. Somebody decided it would be.

---

## The man who wanted scale

Skylar is the director, though he is quick to hand the credit sideways. When I told him the set was fantastic — and it is, a decaying grandeur that reads like *Gone with the Wind* left out in the weather — he corrected me before I finished the sentence. "It's better to say I helped," he said. "Michael took the brunt of that. Michael and Joe. I've been at work a lot."

But the vision is his, and the vision is not small. There is a classic way to stage *Sweeney Todd*, a spare rectangular box that rotates. Skylar knew that box. He was *in* this show fifteen years ago, in this same town, back when it ran with a turning stage and a cast of people some of whom are gone now. He could have reached for the safe version.

"When I decided to take the reins, I just wanted scale," he told me. "I wanted something huge and impactful, because I knew I had the talent that was huge and impactful." He said the word *standard* the way a man says a thing he is a little afraid to want out loud — not a standard he was setting, he insisted, but one he was reaching back toward, the standard of the big shows this community used to mount. *Les Misérables.* The ones you remember for years.

Sondheim, he thinks, wrote his most intricate and his busiest score here. Skylar has gone looking in it. He will tell you the *Dies Irae* — the old Latin chant for the day of wrath — runs under the whole thing as a motif, that Mrs. Lovett's first line may be that chant played backward, that there are layers in every song you could spend hours pulling apart and still find something new. He directs to those motifs, he says, even in the blocking, so the dread gets into you underneath the words. "It enters the subconscious. Even if people don't catch it, it still layers up and builds."

He gave the whole production a private theme and worked it into the costumes, the choreography, the set. He calls it *beauty decaying.* Every character on that stage was beautiful once, or is beautiful still, and every one of them is coming apart — some by their choices, some by their circumstances. Sweeney, obviously. But also Joanna, the innocent, rotting a little inside her cage. "You could apply that to almost any situation," he said, "and still find how it's true for each character."

I have not stopped thinking about it since. It is true for the characters. It is also true for the rest of us in the room.

## The dark and hungry god

The reason I wanted to write about this show is not the razor. It is a question Skylar has been carrying for fifteen years and only recently answered for himself.

*Sweeney Todd* says, in one of its darkest lines, that its barber *serves a dark and a vengeful god,* and later, *a dark and hungry god.* When Skylar first stood inside this show as a younger man, the line snagged him. "I was like, which god are they talking about? Maybe it's some named false god." It is never named. So he sat with it, and the answer he came to is the whole sermon.

"I think the dark and vengeful god he is serving is himself," Skylar said. "He chooses self over everything else."

He is careful about the difference between vengeance and revenge. Sweeney is not, in the end, avenging his wife and daughter, Skylar argues — because he never makes a plan to be reconciled to the daughter he could still save. "He says goodbye to Joanna without any plans to get her back. It's all just, *goodbye, Joanna, and these are my plans.* It's very self-serving. This is what I want. This is what I need to get my own satisfaction." He paused, and then he said the sentence that made me put my pen down. "There are parallels to what we do in our own lives. Maybe not to that extreme."

This is a man of faith directing one of the most violent things ever set to music, and he is not flinching and he is not sanitizing. He told me — this was on the phone, weeks before — that he keeps returning to a prayer he once heard the director pray over a production like this one: *Lord, I pray you somehow get glory out of all this debauchery.* Skylar prays a version of it still.

His theology of the thing is almost Augustine. "I don't think darkness necessarily exists," he said. "Darkness is just the absence of light. You can't bring darkness into a room. You can only bring light. And if you're left with darkness, you're left to your own aimless decisions — the ones that tear your soul apart." Even in the Christian framework, he added, there is no darkness in God at all. So the dark, staged honestly, is not the enemy of the light. It is the outline that lets you see it.

He has a quote he lives by, from the poet Caesar A. Cruz, pinned to his own profile: *Art should comfort the disturbed and disturb the comfortable.* This production is aiming at both, on purpose.

## The man I did not beat

Jason Wright is playing Sweeney.

I should tell you plainly that I auditioned for this show, and that I have known Jason for twenty years, and that watching him step into a role I once carried is a complicated and mostly good thing to feel. I have written elsewhere about the envy and the relief and the grace of it, so I will not relitigate my own small drama here. I will only say that the room made the right call, and that I know it because of how Jason talks about the part.

He struggled with "Epiphany," Sweeney's great unraveling aria — not the notes, the meaning. "I was having such a hard time. I didn't want to do it just how everyone would do it." So he went back to the words, the way he teaches his students to, and something opened. "It's like a salvation moment for him. He's lost his humanity. Everything's been stripped from him. And he unifies with something divine that's past human — a god of vengeance. And he says, *I will have salvation, and the work waits.*" Jason grinned at the horror of it. "It's fun. I don't get to be bad very often."

Jason teaches theater. Half the young people in this cast have called him *sir.* Dax, nineteen, playing Toby; Gracie as Joanna; JoJo, Grayson, Jake — kids he first put on a stage now standing on one beside him. I asked what that was like and he gave me the truest answer anybody gave me all night.

"It's just like teaching with my students," he said. "Every time I get to be in a show with them, it reminds me why I do this. And it gives you more people to love." He turned the phrase over, liking it. "You get gifted more people to love."

He says he feels a little rusty. He is not rusty. He is home.

## The mouthpiece and the maestro

If Jason's Sweeney is stoic and interior, Amanda's Mrs. Lovett does all the talking — and Amanda knows it. She and Skylar were both in that production fifteen years ago; she is stepping now into a role she once watched somebody else play.

"The show is called *Sweeney Todd*," she said, "but Mrs. Lovett is the mouthpiece to his stoicism. She's zero to a hundred all the time." The hard part is not the avalanche of words, though there is that. It is the woman underneath them. "She wants to do anything to please him. She'll do anything to make him hers. You have to be a little off-kilter in the first place to be willing to cross certain lines." Amanda has been chasing the nuance in a character who never slows down. "She's always got a plan. She's thinking ten, twenty years ahead. She knows how to emotionally manipulate people." An enabler, in the end — the one who hands the monster his best idea.

Then there is Silas, the music director, conducting Sondheim for the first time and clearly a little in love with the assignment. He hears the score as a set of arguments carried by the soloists — "Sweeney represents selfishness, Joanna represents purity, Antony represents hope" — with the ensemble used sparingly to hold those ideas up. It is the opposite, he noted, of the last big show he led, *Frozen*, where the crowd pushes the story and the leads punctuate it.

He is honest about how strange the music is. "It's that expressionist period — nonsensical things you're trying to make sense of, and sometimes the best thing you can do is not try. Just do it. Because that's what the story is doing too." Bad things happen, in the plot and in the pit, and you keep moving. Strip away what is harsh to some ears, Silas said, and what is left is lush and thick and full of contrast. "It's humbling to be able to lead it."

## Make room for the dark

Near the end, standing in a hall that smelled like sawdust and fresh paint, twenty-five minutes from the night's rehearsal, we got honest about why any of us make a thing like this.

Jason said it first, the oldest true thing about light: you cannot know it without the dark to walk away from. "If it was all one or the other, there'd be no contrast. You'd never find your way to the light, because there'd be nothing to leave." The whole show, he said, is a mirror and a warning. "Who hasn't told a lie? Who hasn't manipulated somebody? You see little pieces of yourself in these characters — and you also say, *but that's wrong.* It's a morality tale. A cautionary tale."

I gave them the phrase I could not shake. *Hurt people hurt people.* Skylar had heard it that very day, he said, and nodded like it cost him something. I offered the one I hold onto against it — that the things that happen to you, even the ones with no reason attached, ultimately find their place in your life. He liked that better. So do I. It is the sentence Sweeney could never learn to say, and it is the reason the show still works as a warning instead of a wallow.

They are making room for the dark on that stage in Gadsden — a room full of beautiful, decaying people telling the truth about a man who chose himself and called it a god. When the lights come up, some of us will see the razor. A few of us, if Skylar has done what he set out to do, will see the mirror.

I played this part once, before I understood it. These folks understand it. Go watch them.
