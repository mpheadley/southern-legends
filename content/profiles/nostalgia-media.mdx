Most wedding vendors name their business after themselves or after something that sounds elegant and vaguely romantic. Tiffany Debouer named hers after a feeling. That tells you something about what she thinks her job is.

Nostalgia Media is Debouer's wedding photography and videography business. The name is working harder than it looks. Nostalgia is the ache of wanting to return to something you can no longer reach — a moment, a version of a day, a feeling that was present and is now memory. What couples are actually buying when they hire a wedding videographer is not footage. They know it's footage when they sign the contract. But what they want, what they're paying for, is the ability to return to a moment that will otherwise exist only in the unreliable archive of human memory.

The wedding day is one of the most documented days in most people's lives and also one of the least actually experienced. You're moving through it at speed, managing a hundred small logistics, trying to be present while being pulled in every direction. The photographs and video are what you use to actually see what happened. Couples routinely report that watching their wedding video is the first time they saw their own wedding — that the day they were physically present for becomes real to them only in retrospect, through the lens of someone who was watching it instead of living it.

Debouer works in a specific aesthetic tradition that the name "Nostalgia" points toward: warm tones, slower cuts, the look of light that feels like a particular afternoon rather than a bright room. The film-influenced look that has become popular in wedding media over the last decade is popular for a reason — it signals age, memory, the passage of time, the sense that this moment has already become significant. It photographs a day the way that day will feel ten years later.

The practical question couples face when hiring wedding media is not just quality but fit. A videographer who shoots everything brightly and sharply is capturing a different thing than one whose work looks like it was developed in a darkroom, even if it wasn't. Debouer's business name is an honest advertisement for where she sits on that spectrum. She is trying to give couples the feeling they will want to have when they pull out the video on an anniversary and watch it on the couch.

That is a real thing to try to do, and it is harder than it sounds. Anyone can point a camera at a wedding. Catching the moment before the moment becomes memory — that is the work.
