There is a book called *A Guide to Prayer for Ministers and Other Servants*.

It was compiled by Rueben P. Job and Norman Shawchuck, published by Upper Room Books in 1983. It is a lectionary-based devotional for people who do the work of ministry — weekly readings, prayers, poetry, silence. Thomas Merton appears in it. So does John of the Cross. So does nearly every other major figure in the Christian contemplative tradition.

Dale Clem used it.

I found it in seminary — at Northern Seminary in Lombard, Illinois, while I was training as an American Baptist — long before I was serving in United Methodist congregations, and it changed how I understood prayer. The Merton prayer that lives in that book — the one about not knowing where I am going, about trusting that my desire to please God pleases God — I found it there first, before I found it anywhere else. I did not know for years that Dale Clem had been praying with the same book in the same tradition, in the same county where I would eventually walk into a church that used to be his.

That is the thing about formation. The threads run forward and backward in time. You do not see them until much later.

## The Life Before Anniston

Dale Clem grew up in Huntsville. He studied at Birmingham-Southern College and Duke Divinity School. He married [Kelly Clem](/profiles/kelly-clem), who was also called to ministry. On Palm Sunday 1994, their daughter Hannah was killed when an F4 tornado struck Goshen United Methodist Church while Kelly was preaching. Hannah was four years old.

He wrote the book afterward. *Winds of Fury, Circles of Grace: Life After the Palm Sunday Tornadoes*, published by Abingdon Press. It opens with the tornado and Hannah's death, and it is a man's honest account of what faith does and does not do inside the worst thing that can happen to you. It does not resolve cleanly. It was not meant to.

He served at the UMC campus ministry at Jacksonville State University. He pastored Rainbow City First UMC. He served Trinity UMC and Monte Sano UMC in Huntsville. He and Kelly led pilgrimages to Turkey, Greece, and Israel.

## The Anniston Years

Dale Clem's final pastoral appointment was at **Anniston First United Methodist Church**.

He was there for approximately four years, ending with his retirement in 2020, which the *Anniston Star* covered under the headline: *"An advocate for the outcast, Anniston pastor Dale Clem announces retirement."*

He was there to preach at the church that holds [Camp Lee](/essays/camp-lee) on the mountain, the chapel that carries [Martha Vandervoort's](/profiles/martha-vandervoort) name on its wall, the congregation where Kyle Bryan serves as executive director and where, in the spring of 2025, Kyle sat in rocking chairs with [Ciara Smith-Roston](/profiles/ciara-smith-roston) by a stone fireplace while Anniston's future looked back at him.

He was not there when I arrived at that church. But the room he left was still shaped by him.

## The Morning Runs at Camp Lee

Before I resigned, before the mania, before any of the falling — I used to run with Dale Clem in the early mornings.

We would run the mountain roads. Camp Lee is up in the hills above Anniston, and the roads there have the kind of quiet that is hard to find once the day starts. We ran them while it was still dark enough to be honest. Afterward we would sit with coffee and pray. That is the whole thing. Running and coffee and prayer at the lip of the morning, with a man who had survived something I did not yet have a word for and was still showing up.

I did not know yet, in those mornings, what was building in me. I do not think Dale knew either. That is the thing about spiritual friendship in the early hours — it is not diagnostic. It is just presence. Two people deciding the day is worth beginning together.

I think about those mornings now differently than I did then. The mountain road. The coffee going cold. The man across from me who had buried his daughter and kept getting up. I was being formed in a way I could not see. That is usually how formation works.

## What the Book Carried

I want to say something careful about the Guide to Prayer.

I did not know Dale Clem personally when I was a young minister in formation. I bought the book at a seminary bookstore. I prayed with it. I found Thomas Merton's prayer about not knowing where I was going, about the road not being clear, about trusting that my desire to please God was itself a form of pleasing God. That prayer sat in me for twenty years.

Then I ended up in the same county where Dale Clem had served. I learned that he and I had used the same book. That we had both, at different points in our formation, found our way through the contemplative tradition — through Merton, through Job and Shawchuck, through the strange slow practice of praying the hours when everything else was uncertain.

There is a tradition in ministry of formative texts being passed forward without the passer knowing. The books that make ministers make other ministers who make other ministers. The Guide to Prayer is one of them.

Dale Clem used it. I used it. We never met. We served in the same county a decade apart. And somewhere on a shelf in an Anniston house, I am certain the book is still there.
