Anniston used to call itself the Model City. It was a nickname born of genuine ambition — the sense that something being built here was worth pointing at, worth being proud of. That era is complicated history now, like most civic nicknames that come from the early twentieth century. But Julie Tyson reached back into it anyway when she named her glass art business, and there is something quiet and deliberate in that choice.

Model City Glass makes handcrafted glass gifts out of Anniston. Tyson's work lands at The Aisle: Anniston Bridal Show this October in the category of wedding gifts, which is where it belongs — not because glass is traditional, though glass objects have a long history as wedding gifts, but because of what handmade glass objects actually are: permanent things, made slowly, meant to outlast the occasion.

Glass is strange material to work with and to give. It reads as fragile because it is fragile in one specific way — drop it on a hard floor and it will not survive. But glass that is properly made and properly kept doesn't age. It doesn't rust or yellow or swell or shrink. The light moves through it the same way in forty years as it does the day it's given. A handmade glass piece on a windowsill catches the afternoon sun exactly as it did the afternoon a couple carried it home from their reception. That's a different kind of durability than most wedding gifts offer.

The name "Model City" in 2026 is also doing something. Tyson is running a handcraft business in a mid-sized Alabama city that has weathered a great deal. Choosing to call it the Model City isn't nostalgia exactly — it's more like a quiet insistence that the place is worth making things in and making things for. Local craft at a local expo is a coherent position. The object was made here. The couple getting married is getting married here. That line of continuity matters to some people, and it should matter more.

The specific quality of glass as a wedding gift is that it carries light. A ceramic bowl sits on a shelf and holds things. A glass piece changes depending on the time of day, the angle of the sun, the season. It participates in the light of whatever room it's in. That makes it alive in a way that most objects aren't, and it makes it memorable in a way that most wedding gifts aren't.

Tyson will have a selection of her work at the expo. The pieces vary — the specifics of what she brings to October are hers to decide — but the common thread is the same one that runs through any honest handmade object: someone's hands made this, on purpose, for someone to keep.

That's a good reason to pick up something made in Anniston.
