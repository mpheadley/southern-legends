I don't have a first marathon. I have a first half marathon, and Brian Howell ran it with me.

We trained together in 2015 and 2016 — the Mercedes Half Marathon, Birmingham. Early morning miles, the kind of running you do before the day has a temperature opinion. You learn things about a person on long training runs that you would not learn any other way. There is a silence that happens after the third or fourth mile where you stop performing yourself and just move. Brian was good company in that silence.

He traded book recommendations. He had opinions about what he had read, and the opinions were his, not borrowed. That is rarer than it sounds.

---

## The Company

Brian Howell is VP at Auto Custom Carpets — ACC — headquartered in Anniston. His uncle owns the company. ACC is an international operation, not a local novelty: automotive carpet and flooring, original equipment quality, shipped worldwide. It is the kind of company that exists in Anniston largely because it was founded here and stayed, which in manufacturing is its own kind of loyalty.

Before the corporate work, Brian was a county judge. Calhoun County. He has the posture of someone who has had to make decisions in rooms that matter and learned how to hold still while doing it.

---

## The Church Years

Brian married Rebecca Howell — née Sefcik, daughter of Candy and Paul Sefcik, who were both pillars at Weaver First United Methodist Church. Candy Sefcik chaired the board of trustees. The Sefciks were the kind of family a congregation depends on without always naming it. Brian married into that tradition and then became part of it.

He became the SPRC chair — Staff-Parish Relations Committee, the body responsible for the relationship between a congregation and its pastor.

He was chair during 2020.

That means: COVID. Remote services. Livestreams. A congregation that could not gather. And — in the same year — George Floyd. The summer of 2020 asked every American pastor a question: what will you say from the pulpit? The honest answer varied congregation to congregation. In some churches, the question got you a polite request to stay in your lane. In others, it got you a phone call.

Brian was the one who answered the phone if it rang. He was the one who navigated the congregational tension without flinching — or so it looked from where I was standing. I preached those sermons. He managed the room they created. That requires a particular steadiness: the ability to hold the pastor's instincts and the congregation's anxiety in the same hand without squeezing too hard on either.

He held it.

## The Departure

When I left Weaver First for Anniston First in 2021, Brian called it a lateral move.

He was surprised I'd stayed at Weaver as long as I had. He said it without flattery or condescension — just the honest assessment of someone who'd watched from close range and thought I should have seen what he saw earlier.

He was probably right.

The lateral move did not go where either of us expected. Three years later I was out of professional ministry entirely, diagnosed with bipolar disorder, sitting in a UAB psychiatric unit while the flower farm sold. That is not the trajectory we discussed on training runs. But the through-line of it — building something, learning the floor, making room for what came next — is a structure he would recognize.

## The Panel

On August 28, 2024, Brian sat on a panel at the Oxford Civic Center called "United for Hope." [Shannon Jenkins](/profiles/shannon-jenkins) was there. The panel brought together civic and business leaders to talk about the future of northeast Alabama — the kind of conversation that usually sounds better than it lands, but this one had people in the room who had actually done things.

Brian was one of them.

He had been a judge and a corporate VP and an SPRC chair at a church navigating one of the hardest years in American congregational life. He had opinions about the future of the place he lived in, and they were grounded opinions.

I have photos from that night. In most of them, Brian is holding the microphone in the way of a person who has learned that a microphone does not require performance.
