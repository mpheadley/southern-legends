Aubrey Fullbright has a degree in religious studies.

She grew up around metaphysical spaces — they have been part of her life since childhood, not a phase or a discovery but a continuous thread. She knows the traditions, the practices, the particular kind of community that gathers around crystals and herbs and books about things that most Alabama retail does not stock.

During COVID, she got tired of driving to Atlanta or Birmingham to find them.

---

## Why There Was Nothing Here

Calhoun County, Alabama does not have a metaphysical bookshop. Or didn't. The religious culture of Northeast Alabama is predominantly evangelical Protestant — Baptist and Methodist and Pentecostal in their various forms, with Catholic and mainline communities in the mix. A shop selling crystals, incense, Tarot cards, herbs, and books about alternative spiritual traditions is not what the market would have predicted.

But markets don't account for the number of people who are quietly looking for something the dominant religious culture of a place isn't providing, and who have been driving an hour and a half each way to find it.

Aubrey Fullbright counted herself among them. In 2021, she and her husband Leland opened Romarin et Corbeaux.

---

## The Name

*Romarin et Corbeaux.* French: Rosemary and Crows.

The name signals something: this shop is going to be what it is, without apology or translation. A French name in an Alabama strip mall is a statement. It says the place is not embarrassed about what it is. It is not trying to blend in. It is offering something specific, for people who have been looking for it.

---

## Oxford

They first opened at 501 Quintard Avenue in Anniston, then moved to Oxford under a two-year arrangement.

In Oxford, Aubrey Fullbright received death threats.

She has spoken about this publicly — the threats came because of what the shop is and where it is and what some people in Calhoun County think about shops that sell the things hers sells. She named the threats. She described them not to generate sympathy but to locate herself accurately in the landscape of what it is to do this work here.

---

## Noble Street

In June 2024, she and Leland moved back to Anniston. They reopened at 1020 Noble Street.

Noble Street is having a moment in Anniston — the AFUMC Arts and Wellness Center is a few blocks up at 1400, Dr. Danielle Hall from SoundEmbrace hosts sessions in the shop, the corridor is becoming something. Romarin et Corbeaux is part of that becoming: the bookshop that shouldn't exist here by conventional wisdom, on the street where several other things that shouldn't exist are also happening.

---

## What the Shop Is

Crystals, herbs, teas, candles, incense, books. "All things odd and strange" — that is how they describe it. The inventory is ethically sourced. The shop is open Wednesday through Saturday, 10am to 6pm, and Sunday noon to 5pm.

She is also, in the context of her community, an activist — she spoke with the North Meridian Press on "Metaphysical Shops and Activism," connecting the act of running the shop to a broader set of commitments about who gets to exist openly in a place like Calhoun County. The threats she received were not abstract. She stayed.

---

## What Stays

A woman with a degree in religious studies, who grew up with these traditions, got tired of driving, opened a shop, took the threats, moved, and came back.

The French name is still on the window at 1020 Noble Street. Rosemary and Crows. In Anniston, Alabama.
