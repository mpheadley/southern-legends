Pete Conroy is not from Alabama.

He grew up in Pennsylvania. Moved to Asheville, North Carolina in 1970 and trained as a biologist. Bachelor's from Furman, master's in zoology from Georgia. In 1985, he came to Anniston to be curator at the [Anniston Museum of Natural History](/profiles/anniston-museums-gardens).

That was forty years ago. He never left.

---

## What He Built

<Figure src="/images/journal/chief-ladiga-trail.webp" float="center" size="md" alt="The Chief Ladiga Trail" caption="The Chief Ladiga Trail, one of Conroy's projects, runs forty miles from Anniston to the Georgia line." />

Southern Legends is mostly the story of people who left and came back. Conroy inverts that entirely. He came from somewhere else and chose this place so completely that he spent four decades trying to protect and build it.

The [Chief Ladiga Trail](/journal/chief-ladiga-trail) runs forty miles from Anniston to the Georgia line. He had a hand in that. The Little River Canyon National Preserve was designated in 1992. He helped lead that fight. The Little River Canyon Center in Fort Payne opened in 2009, fly fishing and pottery and a covered amphitheater, Canyon Fest every year. He built that too.

Dugger Mountain Wilderness in 2000. Mountain Longleaf National Wildlife Refuge in 2005. A film studio in Jacksonville, Longleaf Studios, the largest green-screen facility in Alabama. The old Fort McClellan grounds in Anniston, found a second use. The mountain center up in Talladega and Cleburne counties, near Centre.

These are federal designations and built facilities across multiple administrations, requiring the kind of coalition work that typically outlasts any single person's patience.

It did not outlast his.

His colleague Ben Shurett called him "the best dreamer and doer." Randy Owen, of the band Alabama, helped on the Little River Canyon work. That tells you something about the range of people Conroy could put in the same room.

<PullQuote>He came from Pennsylvania and gave Northeast Alabama forty years.</PullQuote>

## Six Governors. Two Presidents.

<Figure src="/images/profiles/freedom-riders-national-monument/freedom-riders-mugshots.webp" float="center" size="lg" alt="Freedom Riders" caption="Conroy chairs the Freedom Rider Park Board. The monument was designated in 2017." />

From 1997 until September 2025, Conroy ran JSU's Office of Strategic Partnerships and Special Projects, formerly the Environmental Policy and Information Center. He retired at the end of that September. In January 2026, JSU named him Director Emeritus.

During those years, six Alabama governors appointed him to boards and commissions. Guy Hunt. Jim Folsom. Fob James. Don Siegelman. Bob Riley. Kay Ivey. Republican and Democrat, through four decades of Alabama politics, the same man kept getting the call.

Two presidents appointed him too. Bill Clinton named him Alternate U.S. Federal Commissioner of the Tri-State Water Compacts in 1999. George W. Bush kept him in the role. That fight was partly about the Coosa River and Weiss Lake, the water Alabama shares with Georgia and Florida and has been arguing about ever since.

<PullQuote>Six governors and two presidents, both parties, appointed the same man.</PullQuote>

In 2017, the [Freedom Riders National Monument](/profiles/freedom-riders-national-monument) was designated. Conroy helped lead that work too. The monument sits near Anniston, where the bus was firebombed in 1961. He now chairs the Freedom Rider Park Board, Inc.

The man who protected the longleaf pines and the man who preserved the site of that burning are the same man.

## The Contested Ground

<Figure src="/images/journal/chief-ladiga-trail-train-station.webp" float="center" size="md" alt="Anniston depot along the Chief Ladiga Trail" />

In 2022, JSU's Longleaf Studios produced a documentary, "The Forgotten Creeks," about the Poarch Band of Creek Indians. The Poarch are the Mvskoke Creeks who stayed in Alabama after the Trail of Tears and won federal recognition as a sovereign nation in 1984. The documentary aired on Alabama Public Television.

Conroy said it was a real honor. As an APT commissioner, a JSU employee, and someone who had known the Poarch people for nearly thirty years, he had helped connect the institutions that made the film.

This is where it gets complicated. Not with Conroy, but with the ground itself.

<PhotoCarousel slidesId="pete-conroy" />

The Muscogee (Creek) Nation, the tribal nation removed to Oklahoma, and the Poarch Band have been in a long dispute since 2012 over Hickory Ground, Oce Vpofv, a sacred site in Wetumpka where ancestral remains were disturbed during casino construction. A documentary honoring the Poarch is never a neutral thing inside Creek country. The same land can be a preserve to one people and a wound to another.

This is not an accusation. Conroy has worked in conservation and history and politics long enough to know that honest work runs across contested ground. He built a trail named for [who Chief Ladiga was](/journal/who-was-chief-ladiga), a Creek leader who tried to stay. He helped tell the story of [what remained of the Creek Nation](/journal/muscogee-creek-nation-what-remained) in Alabama. Neither of those was simple work.

It never was.

## Still Showing Up

I talked with Pete Conroy recently about the Chief Ladiga Trail. He was warm and straightforward. I went to college at JSU. I farmed flowers off that trail for years, at a kiosk in Weaver, where I also pastored a church for seven years. The trail corridor runs right through there.

He is Special Advisor at the Little River Canyon Center now. Still serving on the Alabama Educational Television Commission. Still chairing the Freedom Rider Park Board. He won the River Hero Award in 2015. The Alabama Tourism Department gave him a Director's Award in 2024.

His wife Roxana is in Jacksonville with him. His daughter Haley is in Centre. His son Mitch is close by.

<PullQuote>He came from somewhere else. He chose this place. He kept choosing it.</PullQuote>

Southern Legends runs on the diaspora story, the people who left and what they brought back with them. Conroy is the other version of that. He arrived from outside and put down roots so deep that six governors trusted him and two presidents called his name.

He did not have to stay. He was not from here. That is the part that matters.

---

*Pete Conroy is Director Emeritus of Jacksonville State University's Office of Strategic Partnerships and Special Projects. He arrived in Alabama in 1985 and never left.*
