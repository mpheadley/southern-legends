From the Publisher

I lost a farm. I was hospitalized. We rebuilt in my in-laws' yard with a converted horse trailer that used to be a hat bar in Florida. Southern Legends is what came out of the other side of that — a magazine about people in Northeast Alabama who are still building something, made by someone who knows what it costs not to.

I am also the founder and creative director of The Aisle, a curated bridal show at the Anniston Museums and Gardens on October 18. The Bloom Bar — that horse trailer — is the centerpiece.

I build websites and software for local businesses. I direct the Anniston Farmers Market. I am a pastor who doesn't work at a church. All of this is connected. I'm still figuring out how.

If something in these pages sent you looking for more, here's where to find it.

Essay
The Same Domain

I've been silent for six months. No Facebook posts. No calls or texts. Just withdrew. And missed people the whole time. I wanted to reconnect. I had a community, a network. I didn't want to lose that, but I felt like I had.

I walk up and down Littlejohn Road in Pleasant Valley a few times a day. I walk past farms and gardens and fences and houses. Things people have that I had, that I built, or that I was planning to build. There's still hard feelings about that. Heather and I talk about it when we walk.

I still hesitate before looking at the farms and gardens. I look at them anyway.

Southern Legends was conceived as a top-of-the-funnel strategy for finding web design clients. Something was blocking me. Writing about business owners that were succeeding where I had failed. The comparison was too hard. So I pocketed the idea and did cold calling instead.

Getting hung up on sucks.

After basking in rejection, I started asking for referrals. Texted fifty friends: "Hey, it's Matt, hope you're doing well. I'm stable now and doing web design. You know anybody?" It pulled me out.

After the texts, I finally started to feel ready to write. I reached out to Samuel and Jean. After writing and sharing them, people read and commented. I needed some wins. This was a way to win before I had any clients.

I don't have a pulpit anymore. But I still need a place to process publicly.

Some clients could be turned off by my history. Bipolar. Hospitalization. Business failure. Maybe those aren't the best clients anyway.

Maybe being broken is what makes the profiles work.

Both the journal and the profiles, on the same domain.

The Burning Bus
On May 14, 1961, a Greyhound bus was firebombed outside Anniston, Alabama. The photograph that ran in papers around the world was taken right here. Sixty years later, the place is a national monument two blocks from a hardware store that has been in the same family since 1963.
Freedom Riders National Monument  ·  Anniston, Alabama
southernlegends.us
The Burning Bus9

The first thing most people know about the Freedom Rides is the photograph. A Greyhound bus on the shoulder of a two-lane road, smoke pouring from the windows, a crowd standing at a distance. It was taken outside Anniston, Alabama, on May 14, 1961 — Mother's Day. The photograph ran on front pages in forty countries. It became one of the defining images of the American civil rights movement. The place where it was taken is now a national monument, two blocks from a hardware store.

The Freedom Riders were a group of Black and white activists who boarded interstate buses in Washington, D.C., on May 4, 1961, testing the Supreme Court's rulings desegregating interstate travel facilities. They expected resistance. They got it at every stop. But nothing prepared them for Anniston.

I.
May 14, 1961

The Greyhound bus arrived in Anniston that Sunday afternoon and was immediately surrounded by a mob of two hundred people. Some were armed with clubs and iron pipes. The bus driver tried to leave. The mob slashed the tires. The driver managed to pull onto the highway heading east, toward Bynum, the crowd following in cars.

Six miles outside of town, the tires gave out. The bus limped to a stop on Highway 202. The mob caught up. Someone threw an incendiary device through a window. The bus filled with smoke. Riders were trapped.

When the doors finally opened, passengers stumbled out into a crowd that beat them as they fell. The Anniston police were nowhere to be seen. The local fire department arrived and stood by while the bus burned. An Alabama state investigator was present — undercover, sent to protect the riders. He fired his pistol into the air to scatter the crowd long enough for people to get clear of the bus before the fuel tank exploded.

"The local police had advance notice the bus was coming. They chose not to be there."
II.
The Burning
Highway 202, May 14, 1961. NPS

Janie Miller, a Black woman who lived near the roadside where the bus stopped, opened her home to the riders who had stumbled out. She gave them water. She called for help. Some of the riders were hospitalized at Anniston Memorial Hospital — now Regional Medical Center, where my children were born — after staff initially refused to treat them.

A second bus — a Trailways — had also traveled through Anniston that day. On that bus, a group of white men boarded in Atlanta and beat the Freedom Riders before the bus even reached Alabama. When the Trailways arrived in Anniston, riders were forced off and attacked. Walter Bergman, a retired professor, was beaten so severely that he suffered a stroke. He never fully recovered.

The Anniston police had advance notice the bus was coming. They had been in contact with the Ku Klux Klan. They chose not to be there when the mob was waiting.

The Burning Bus11
III.
What It Changed
Freedom Riders arrested in Mississippi, 1961. NPS

The photograph of the burning Greyhound ran worldwide the following morning. The Kennedy administration, already trying to manage the optics of the Cold War, could not ignore it. Attorney General Robert Kennedy called for a "cooling off period." The Freedom Riders refused.

Over the following months, more than four hundred people participated in Freedom Rides across the South. They were beaten in Birmingham. They were jailed in Mississippi. The rides continued until November 1961, when the Interstate Commerce Commission issued regulations desegregating interstate travel facilities — the ruling the original Rides had set out to enforce.

"This wasn't a failure of the system. This was the system working exactly as it had been designed to work. The Freedom Riders didn't ask the system to fix itself. They made the cost of maintaining it too high to ignore."

The ICC regulations didn't end segregation in the South. But they marked a turning point in the strategy of the civil rights movement: nonviolent direct action, staged for maximum visibility, designed to force federal intervention. Anniston was that strategy's most photographed moment.

11
The Burning Bus12
IV.
The Monument

In January 2017, President Obama designated the Freedom Riders National Monument in Anniston, Alabama — one of his final acts before leaving office. The monument encompasses two sites: the Greyhound bus station on Gurnee Avenue, where the Anniston attacks began, and the roadside site on Highway 202 where the bus burned.

I walked past the Gurnee Avenue station for four years without knowing what it was. It was just part of downtown — a boarded-up storefront, brick front, a marker I never stopped to read. My kids were born two blocks away at Regional Medical Center, in the same hospital where the riders were initially refused treatment in 1961.

Next door at 1030 Gurnee, Downing and Sons has been in the same family since 1963 — the same year the store opened, two years after the attack. Lewis Downing has run his family's business in that shadow his whole adult life. His profile begins on page 23.

V.
Why It Matters Now

The national monument designation doesn't change what happened. It doesn't undo the silence of the police, the beaten bodies, the burned bus. What it does is mark the place as a site of consequence — a place where the cost of the system became impossible to look away from. That seems worth remembering, especially in a town still reckoning with what it has been and what it is trying to become.

The View from the Boat
Jean Ellison spent twenty years at JSU. Then her second child was born, her car was repossessed, a friend sat with her on the floor, and she built a restaurant on Noble Street. The Music Box has been open ever since.
Jean Ellison  ·  Mom-To-Go & The Music Box  ·  Anniston, Alabama
southernlegends.us
The View from the Boat14

Jean Ellison had a plan. She'd spent twenty years at Jacksonville State University — teaching, performing, building a life in the community. She knew the music scene. She knew the food scene. She'd watched the Noble Street corridor come back slowly, awkwardly, the way downtowns do. She had a vision for what a restaurant could be in a space like that. She had the timeline worked out.

Then her second child was born, and she learned something she says she should have known twenty years earlier: when you plan, God laughs.

The joke Jean tells about herself is that she used to give advice about postpartum anxiety before she'd ever experienced it herself. She was the person in the room who would say, gently, have you considered that this might be more than fatigue? She'd taken the training. She knew the clinical language. She was, she says, "completely full of it."

I.
Welcome to the Club

After her second child — Maverick — was born, Jean didn't recognize herself. The training she'd had didn't prepare her for what it actually felt like to be inside it. She describes the exhaustion as something distinct from tiredness: a kind of static that sat behind her eyes and made every ordinary thing feel like a second language.

She kept teaching. She kept performing. She kept showing up. She told herself it was just hard for everyone and that she was no different and that she just needed to push through.

14
The View from the Boat15
Jean outside The Music Box, Noble Street.

I told her about my own year — the diagnosis, the farm, rebuilding from the ground up after a manic episode that cost more than money. She nodded. "Welcome to the club," she said. "Nobody wants to be in it. Everybody in it understands everybody else."

Jean had been teaching at Weaver High School by then — a school where, she says, a significant number of students came in hungry. Not hungry like they'd skipped breakfast. Hungry like they weren't sure what dinner was going to be.

"Steven looked at me one night and said, 'You know you don't have to be okay, right?' I didn't know that. I genuinely did not know that."

"The walls started moving," is how she describes the period after Maverick was born. She means it metaphorically, mostly. The structure she'd built — JSU, the teaching, the music — was still technically standing. But something underneath it had shifted, and she didn't have language for what it was or how to talk about it, even though she'd spent years developing exactly that kind of language for other people.

15
The View from the Boat16
II.
The Floor

She kept denying the exhaustion. Sleep deprivation felt like a badge people wore proudly in early parenthood and she was wearing it too, only she couldn't figure out why the badge felt like it was made of lead. The car payment fell behind. Then the Altima was repossessed. She came out one morning and it was gone. That was the morning she stopped pretending.

"There were days in the nursery," she says, "where I would just sit in the chair and think, I do not know how to do this." The chair is specific to her — a particular rocking chair in a particular nursery — and the specificity of it is the thing that made her tell me about it. Not the feelings in the abstract. The chair. The window. The time of day.

A friend came over — Jean describes her as a "friend-therapist," by which she means a friend who happened to have the training and who sat down on the floor with her, not across from her in a professional chair, not with a clipboard, but on the floor. They stayed there for a while. Jean says that was the thing that mattered: not the words, but the floor.

"From that floor, The Music Box was born." She says it without drama. It took several years and a lot of work and Steven building most of it himself. But the decision — the one that said I am going to make something instead of disappear into this — got made on that floor.

The Music Box opened on Noble Street in downtown Anniston. The space has the feel of somewhere that has been cared for by someone who knows what it is like to need a place to land.

16
The View from the Boat17
III.
What She's Building
Jean Ellison in her kitchen.

Jean is explicit that she does not want to be a resilience symbol. She finds the frame limiting. "People want to know how I survived it so they can extract the formula," she says. "But it wasn't a formula. It was a floor and a friend and a lot of time."

When I ask about the future, she is direct: "I want to be a millionaire." She says it plainly, without apology. Not because money is the point, but because she understands that financial stability is what makes all the other things possible — the restaurant, the cookbook, the work with students, the ability to build something her kids can run with someday.

"I want to be a millionaire. I'm not embarrassed to say that. I know what it felt like to have the car repossessed. I don't want to feel that again."

Maverick was born at 8 in the morning. By 2 that afternoon, Jean was asking the nurse about her regimen for getting back in shape. The nurse laughed. Jean didn't. "I had things to do," she says. The cookbook is in progress. The Music Box is on Noble Street. The walls have stopped moving.

A New Way of Growing
Samuel and John Mark Sawyer are growing 24,000 heads of lettuce and 300 pounds of mushrooms in a former pharmacy warehouse in downtown Anniston. They have no soil and no pesticides. They have a five-year plan and a mission statement. They are very serious about both.
Samuel & John Mark Sawyer  ·  Aquality Farms  ·  Anniston, Alabama
southernlegends.us
A New Way of Growing19

The first time I saw Samuel Sawyer at the farmers market, he had two white coolers and no booth representative. He was pouring samples of something green and drinkable from a pitcher, explaining the nutritional content to whoever would stop long enough to listen. A couple of kids took cups. He told them what was in it. They drank it anyway.

This was before I knew what Aquality Farms was. I knew Samuel as a customer — he and his father John Mark had bought flowers from Heather for their Harvest Box CSA subscription, and I knew they were doing something with mushroom compost that Heather found interesting. I didn't know I was watching the beginning of something.

Later, Samuel showed me a napkin. It had five years of research on it — yield projections, nutrient density comparisons, market analysis. The handwriting was small and deliberate. This was not a hobby that had gotten out of hand. This was a plan.

I.
The Numbers

Aquality Farms currently grows 24,000 heads of lettuce per growing cycle, 300 pounds of mushrooms per month, across 6,000 square feet of vertical hydroponic towers in a repurposed warehouse in downtown Anniston. There is no soil. There are no pesticides. The water is recirculated. The lights are calibrated to specific spectrums for different growth phases.

None of this happened quickly. Samuel spent six years studying hydroponics before breaking ground. "I didn't want to start before I knew what I was doing," he says. "I wanted to build something that actually worked."

19
A New Way of Growing20
II.
The Warehouse
No soil, no pesticides.

The Sawyer family has been in Anniston for thirty years. The building they now farm in operated as a pharmacy from 1969 to 1999. After the pharmacy closed, it sat. Downtown Anniston has a lot of buildings that sat.

Samuel and John Mark took the space and built up, not out. The hydroponic towers rise in rows under blue-spectrum grow lights that hum at a frequency that takes a few minutes to stop noticing. The mushroom lab is in a separate room — temperature controlled, humidity controlled, dark except for the blue-spectrum lights aimed at the pinning boards where the chestnut mushrooms begin to form.

John Mark handles most of the mechanical engineering. Samuel handles the growing science and the outreach. The division of labor was natural, they say — they've always thought in different registers. "He sees structures," Samuel says of his father. "I see systems."

The vertical towers allow them to stack growing cycles and control nutrient delivery with a precision that field farming can't match. A head of butterhead lettuce from Aquality Farms takes about three weeks from seed to harvest. A comparable head from a conventional operation might travel 1,500 miles to reach the same shelf.

20
A New Way of Growing21
III.
The Mission
Chestnut mushrooms pinning under blue-spectrum lights.

When I ask Samuel why he's doing this, his answer centers on health — specifically, on the 35,000 students in the Anniston City and Calhoun County school systems who eat lunch in buildings where the produce options are what budget and supply chain allow.

The Harvest Box CSA program delivers fresh produce — much of it from Aquality — directly to subscribers. The School Sponsorship Program allows individuals and businesses to fund produce delivery to a specific school. "The kids who are eating what we grow are eating things they wouldn't have access to otherwise," Samuel says. "That matters to me more than the margin."

"The fear of failure is real. But the fear of not trying — of looking back and knowing I had a chance to do something that mattered and I didn't take it — that's worse."

"Chaha Outdoors" is the Creek word for what they're building beyond the warehouse — a vision that includes trail partnerships with NAWA, the Native American Wellness Alliance, and outdoor programming connected to the Chaha Trail network. Samuel is Creek. The name is intentional.

Every new business that opens downtown Anniston is a bet on a city that has been told, in various ways, for a long time, that it is not worth betting on. The Sawyers are placing their bet in a former pharmacy. They are growing lettuce under blue lights. They are very serious about it.

People · Community
1030 Gurnee
Lewis Downing runs Downing and Sons on Gurnee Avenue. His grandfather bought the building for $600 in 1963. The Freedom Riders National Monument is next door.
Lewis Downing  ·  Anniston, Alabama
southernlegends.us
1030 Gurnee24

The music director was roaming the neighborhood again. That's how I first encountered him — a figure moving through the block with the unhurried confidence of someone who had been walking these streets longer than I had been alive. He wore a straw hat. He paused outside a brick-front building. Plants sat in the windows. He went inside.

That was R.D. — Robert Downing. And the building was 1030 Gurnee Avenue, which has belonged to the Downing family since 1963, which is when R.D.'s father walked across the street and bought it for six hundred dollars.

"That was Johnny Bullock's place," Lewis tells me, explaining what Gurnee looked like when his grandfather was navigating it. The tools visible just inside the door include a stirrup hoe and a selection of seed packets. Lewis Downing is the third generation to stand behind that counter.

I.
The $600 Store

1030 Gurnee has been a Downing family operation since 1963 — the year Lewis's grandfather walked across the street, met the owner, and bought the building outright for $600. The anchor tenant at the time was Mary Carter Paint. When Mary Carter went bankrupt, the Downings had a building, a customer base, and a decision to make about what came next.

What came next was: everything that would fit. Lewis's grandmother added sewing notions and fabric. The store began carrying organic fertilizer. It acquired upholstery supplies. "Seed packets and upholstery fabric in the same building," Lewis says. The combination sounds improbable. It also made them, he observes, essentially impossible to replace. There is no chain store that carries what they carry. There is no Amazon algorithm that recreates the experience of asking Lewis Downing a question about polyurethane foam.

24
1030 Gurnee25
Lewis Downing. Illustration by Matt Headley.

Lewis took full ownership of Downing and Sons in 2017. In the years since, he has doubled the store's growth. The inventory now includes European upholstery fabrics and leather hides alongside the organic fertilizer and the seed packets. He cuts polyurethane foam forms to specification in the back. He sets the plants out at a quarter of eight every morning, the same time his grandfather did.

He started at the University of Alabama. He transferred to Jacksonville State. Both times, the store pulled him back. "I wasn't ready to leave it," he says. Whether he means the store or the city is unclear, and I don't think the distinction matters much to him.

II.
The Alley

The address is 1030 Gurnee Avenue. Next door, across an alley, is 1031 — the Greyhound bus station where the Freedom Riders were attacked on May 14, 1961. That alley is now part of the Freedom Riders National Monument.

I walked past that alley every week for four years while living and working in downtown Anniston. It was just part of the block — a gap between buildings, unremarkable until you stop. Lewis Downing has run his family's business in that shadow his entire adult life.

He supports the historic district designation that would formalize protection for the corridor. "It matters what this block is recognized as," he says. He doesn't elaborate. He doesn't need to.

25
1030 Gurnee26
III.
R.D.
Fall mums outside Downing and Sons.

Robert Downing — R.D. — served sixteen years as a Calhoun County Commissioner from 1994 to 2010. He was known as the commission's resident environmentalist, in the way that a person becomes known for caring about something others consider optional. He pushed energy efficiency programs that saved the county an estimated $50,000 per year. He worked on the McClellan Joint Powers Authority as the former Army base was being converted. He walked into Downing and Sons on most days, the way family does.

When Heather and I were selling flowers at the farmers market, R.D. and Lewis would come by. R.D. would walk the booths without particular urgency, just perusing. "He'd already been a county commissioner by then," Lewis says. "But he'd make his way over and walk the booths." He bought plants. They were regular customers.

IV.
The Farm Years

One year, the farmers market was held in R.D.'s alleyway. He hosted it. Both of them — R.D. and Lewis — would come on market days and buy flowers and eggs and whatever else the vendors had. We patronized them; they patronized us. That is the economy of a small market, and it works because everybody in it understands that.

Lewis gave us a brown envelope of Purple Hull pea seeds that spring. He told us when to plant them. We did. They came up. "Farming is hard," he said once, standing at our booth, which was not an observation directed at us so much as at the general situation.

26
1030 Gurnee27
V.
The Bible

In December 2024, Lewis Downing was appointed unanimously to the Anniston City Council representing Ward 1. He was sworn in on his grandmother's Bible — an early 1920s edition, the same Bible his father used when he was sworn into the county commission. R.D. was in the room.

His first official act as a council member was to move to adjourn the meeting. He ran for the full term in August 2025 and won in a landslide.

He now serves as Vice Mayor alongside Mayor Ciara Smith-Roston, who is both the city's first Black female mayor and its youngest ever. Ward 1 includes downtown Anniston and 18,000 acres of the former Fort McClellan installation. Lewis chairs Main Street Anniston.

He has said he ran because of a milestone birthday — because he reached a point where he wanted to be an asset and not a liability. He did not say it as a slogan. He said it the way a person says something they have actually thought about.

Every morning at a quarter of eight, he sets the plants out in front of 1030 Gurnee Avenue, next door to a national monument, in a building his grandfather bought for $600.

Community · Faith
When the Churches Stopped Competing
Since 1975, local congregations in Calhoun County have pooled what they have to serve people in crisis. No single church could do it alone. Together, they do.
Interfaith Ministries of Calhoun County  ·  Anniston, Alabama
southernlegends.us
When the Churches Stopped Competing30
Illustration by Matt Headley.

I spent four years as associate pastor at First United Methodist Church in Anniston. The office I worked in was on the second floor, overlooking Gurnee Avenue and the parking lot and, if you stood at the right window, the alleyway next door that would eventually become part of the Freedom Riders National Monument. Interfaith Ministries operated out of a building on the same campus. Some mornings, I would hear the people waiting for Interfaith's doors to open before I saw them — voices in the parking lot, the sound of paper cups, the particular early-morning quiet of people who have learned to wait.

In 1975, a group of Protestant pastors, Catholic priests, and members of Temple Beth El sat down together. The question on the table was simple: "Why are we all trying to do the same thing separately?" Each congregation had some version of an emergency assistance fund. Each had some version of a food pantry. Each had some version of a list of people in crisis who needed help they didn't have the capacity to provide alone.

"Why are we all trying to do the same thing separately?"

By 1978, Interfaith Ministries of Calhoun County was incorporated. Baptist and Catholic, Black and white, downtown and rural, Christian and Jewish — sitting at the same table, pooling what they had. The model still works.

30
When the Churches Stopped Competing31
I.
The Man in the Wheelchair
Illustration by Matt Headley.

There was a man who came through the corridor outside the church several times a week. Long hair, gentle manner, soft-spoken. He had been a hair stylist. He was in a wheelchair. We had several conversations — on 14th Street, on Noble Street, in the parking lot. He was the kind of person who made conversation easy, which is not something you can say about everyone you meet in a parking lot at 7 in the morning.

Then I stopped seeing him. Months passed. One day, Kyle — one of the Interfaith staff — sent me a text: "I thought you would want to know, because I knew you developed a relationship with him."

"I thought you would want to know, because I knew you developed a relationship with him."

Shepherd's Table is Interfaith's weekly community breakfast — a legacy of Dale Clem, a former First UMC pastor who started it decades ago. I led it for four years. We served sausage and pancakes and muffins. My own children came and helped on Saturday mornings. There was a man who wrote poetry in pencil on notebook paper and tore off copies to give away. There was a woman named Kaffie who said "blessed rising" when she arrived and who would clap along if you played guitar.

31
When the Churches Stopped Competing32
II.
Eight Programs

Interfaith runs eight programs: Emergency Assistance, Shepherd's Table, Adult Dental, Meals on Wheels, The Open Door, SenioRx, Christmas Clearing House, Durable Medical Equipment, and Integrative Health Coaching. The Emergency Assistance program is the one most people think of when they think of crisis intervention — a family facing eviction, a utility about to be cut off, a gap between paychecks and rent.

"That's not a situation where spiritual counsel helps. It's a situation where money helps."

The executive director says this without apology. The churches that fund Interfaith have learned, over five decades, that the most effective form of pastoral care is sometimes a check that covers the difference between someone staying housed and someone losing everything. Theology can wait. The utility shutoff cannot.

There was a man named Taz. I saw him on the bench in the courtyard garden most mornings I was at the church — he was drunk every time I saw him, which was not a judgment, just an observation. One morning I ran into April, who coordinates some of Interfaith's case management work, at a coffee shop. She told me Taz was living with a family member now. No longer homeless. "That's the kind of news you don't expect," she said. I asked what had changed. She shrugged. "People are complicated. Sometimes things shift."

32
When the Churches Stopped Competing33
III.
The Coalition

The coalition that Interfaith represents has held for fifty years because the people involved have been willing to set aside the things that make congregations competitive — membership numbers, brand identity, the particular theology of who gets to help whom — and keep focus on the thing they all agreed on in 1975: that no single church could do it alone.

Government programs have eligibility requirements. Nonprofits have funding cycles. Grant-funded initiatives have sunset dates. Interfaith has been open four days a week for half a century. "This is the work that doesn't make the news," the director says. "There's no ribbon-cutting when a family avoids eviction."

During my first bipolar depression I was afraid I'd become one of these people with mental health disorders.

I mean that sentence in the flat, clinical way it sounds, not as a confession of stigma I've since resolved. I was afraid. The fear was specific and real. And the people I was afraid of becoming were people I then spent four years sitting across from at a breakfast table on Saturday mornings, and what I learned — slowly, incompletely — is that the thing I was afraid of was not what I thought it was.

The Hospital37

I was inside pacing, peering out our kitchen window. Shoppers were picking through our stuff. Kids were moping that they had to be out there all morning. Heather and my in-laws were talking to shoppers and neighbors. I couldn't go out.

Days before, we were clearing out the farm. Heather dragged me outside to help. Farm supplies. Tools. Tangles of drip irrigation and electric fence netting. A half-finished perimeter fence. Piles of farm trash.

The mania had ended. The depression that followed had been building for months.

Riding shotgun in Dad's Silverado, on our second trip to the dump, he said it. "Buddy, it's time for you to go to the hospital. I know you don't want to. But I don't want my grandkids to lose their dad."

Heather called Kyle. I sat on the porch with him, looking over our flower field. He'd preached recently about his own hospital stay. He called it his "grippy socks vacation." I agreed to let them drive me to Birmingham.

We parked, walked to UAB. They waited with me in the ER for six hours. The psychiatrist asked if I wanted to be admitted to the residential program. "I do not." I left with a spring in my step.

The next day, I called Dad to take me back.

I.
Ten Days

I was in the psych center for ten days. I told people, and myself, that it had been three weeks. I was sure, until I checked the journal.

I found it in my bedside drawer. A cheap brown notebook with lined paper. I wrote with the golf pencil they gave me — less dangerous than a full-length one.

The Hospital38
The notebook. UAB, October 2025.

Sterile hospital air. The Indian girl who was kind, also bipolar. Stellar carrot cake. I learned from the others that you had to write it on the meal card, though they occasionally got the meals wrong.

The ones desperate to leave, yelling and crying on the phone. The schizophrenic guy talking to imaginary people. The ones alone in their rooms down the hall, laughing and carrying on conversations. I remember all of them.

I journaled: "I used to run, strength train, bike to work and write sermons." Feeling trapped, walking in circles. The shower dribbling water, huddling in the corner to get wet. The yoga mat calling me. Snack time: pb crackers, Oreos. Stepping out at 1 a.m. for sleep meds and pb crackers.

Dr. Bhatia

A couple of mornings a week they brought us individually into Dr. Bhatia's office. She sat in the center of the room, the large window to the hospital courtyard behind her, surrounded by three training psychiatrists and the social worker, big table in a small room. Looking in her eyes across the table. "It sounds like you've tried a lot of different medications. That's bad practice — cycling through them before reaching maximum therapeutic effect." She was talking about my psychiatrist. I didn't know who was right.

I read a book someone left behind — The Butlerian Jihad, a late prequel in the Dune series. Talked about it with one of the attending psychiatrists and with my roommate Pete, who had read some of the series too. I couldn't read it when I got home, even though I'd had Mom bring it to me while still inside after I finished the Jihad.

The Visits

Heather took all my calls while I was in there. She started her new job at the museum, while packing boxes, selling furniture on Marketplace, homeschooling the kids. Pete
Littlejohn Road, winter 2025.

Last Easter I was manic. We went to Anniston First, where I used to be associate pastor. Sporting purple pants and a pink tie. Early in the service I slipped out to the narthex for snacks, coffee, and handshakes with latecomers. A strange freedom, not leading from the front. I joined Heather in the pews near the back. Anniston First United Methodist Church. The screen said what it said.

I had resigned the first week of Lent. That morning we sat the kids down in our living room. The kids cried. Are we leaving the church? We said no. Then I FaceTimed Jason. He was busy teaching but he answered. "I know you're doing this because you feel like you have to," he said. "I'm happy for you, and I'll be praying for you." Took the kids to IHOP.

The surgery was finally scheduled after a year of waiting. Credit card bills piling up. Letting go of staff we'd taken on a month or two earlier. Then the weight of depression started to return.

Chief Ladiga, 1832
"I used to call it 'la-dee-ga.' I didn't know how else to say it. I also didn't know whose land I was running on."
Chief Ladiga, Muscogee Creek leader, c. 1832.

There are trails you run to get fit, and trails you run to get away, and occasionally there is a trail that becomes the shape of a decade. The Chief Ladiga Trail is 33 miles of rail corridor between Anniston and Piedmont, Alabama, along Cedar Springs Creek. I've been on it in every season, in every kind of light, and it has been the background of more of my interior life than I can fully account for.

I used to call it "la-dee-ga." I heard it that way, or I made it up because I didn't know. It wasn't until I started writing this that I learned it's "lah-DEE-gah" — the name of a Muscogee Creek chief who was removed from this land under the Indian Removal Act of 1832. He signed the Treaty of Cusseta. The land I was running on was his. I didn't know that. For years I didn't know that.

I was at JSU when I first found the trail. I had an apartment that backed up to the corridor before it was fully paved. Heather and I were dating. We ran it. Then I was at the Weaver parsonage for seven years — a house that sits practically on top of the trail, close enough that I could hear the creek from the back steps. I wrote sermons in that house. I ran the trail between drafts. I cried a lot on that trail, as I remember.

52
Excerpt — Essay53

Noah was maybe four. We were on the trail near the Weaver section on a morning in April — pink mimosa blooms, morning dew still on the gravel, the particular hush of early spring. He pointed at a plastic bag caught in the fence at the trail edge. "Why do people leave trash?" he asked. I said I didn't know. He thought about it. "Because they're broken," he said.

"Because they're broken."

I did not have a better answer. I still don't.

We moved to the farm — a property in Pleasant Valley that sits on the Choccolocco Creek watershed, right there on the trail corridor. For years there had been talk of extending the trail south through that section, behind the Walmart distribution center, along the creek. I watched the clearing from our property. I technically trespassed on the right-of-way before it was paved, walking the gravel bed of what would eventually become a finished trail.

The grand opening was on a rainy Saturday. Ciara Smith was vice mayor then. She gave a speech. Someone cut a ribbon. "Four million dollars," somebody near me said, reading the signage. I stood in the rain thinking about the number of times I had already walked this section illegally and whether that made me an early adopter or a trespasser.

Later — after the farm, after the hospitalization, after the rebuilding — I was on the trail near the trailhead and Mayor Ciara Smith and Patrick rode up on bikes and stopped. "Hey Matt!" They asked what I was doing. I had a post-hole digger. It's a long story. She laughed. Some things don't need explanation if you've lived in a place long enough.

About Southern Legends
Southern Legends is an independent literary journalism publication from Anniston, Alabama. Each edition profiles the people, places, and institutions of Northeast Alabama — not as inspiration pieces, but as honest attention paid to people who are still building something in a place that gets overlooked.

The editorial lens: Matt Headley lost a farm and now builds websites for local businesses. The work keeps putting him across the table from people who are still going, and he notices them because of what it cost him not to. The question underneath every profile isn't "what does this person do?" It's "what kept them going?" — asked by someone who wanted to keep going and couldn't, for a while.

Southern Legends publishes annually. Stories appear first at southernlegends.us before appearing in print.

About The Aisle
The Aisle is the wedding vendor platform and bridal show for Eastern Alabama and West Georgia. The inaugural expo takes place October 18, 2026, at the Anniston Museums and Gardens. The Bloom Bar is the centerpiece.

The Aisle connects couples planning their weddings with the photographers, florists, caterers, officiants, and venues in their own backyard. Booth registration is open.
